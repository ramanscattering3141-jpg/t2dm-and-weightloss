Type 2 diabetes and attenuated weight loss during incretin-based therapy: mechanisms linking glucose homeostasis to energy balance

Abstract

Clinical trials of incretin-based therapies consistently report smaller reductions in body weight in people with type 2 diabetes (T2D) than in people without diabetes. We asked how robust that observation is and which mechanisms can account for it. We identified nine comparisons in which the same molecule, dose, treatment duration and estimand were tested in separate populations with and without T2D: semaglutide (three programmes, including semaglutide 7.2 mg in STEP UP and STEP UP T2D), tirzepatide (two doses), orforglipron (two doses), cagrilintide–semaglutide and maridebart cafraglutide. In these comparisons the placebo-adjusted weight reduction in T2D was a median 0.63 (range 0.50–0.78) of that in populations without diabetes, an absolute difference of 2–7 percentage points. A prespecified pooled analysis of harmonised semaglutide heart-failure trials confirmed strong heterogeneity between the diabetes and non-diabetes populations (I² ≈ 96%). The deficit is graded across normoglycaemia, prediabetes and T2D, and it appears across agents with different receptor profiles. Placebo-arm weight change is not smaller in T2D, which places the deficit in the drug-attributable effect. Human studies show that incretin agonists suppress appetite in T2D, but no study has compared the magnitude of that suppression with that in people without diabetes. The peripheral apparatus around the receptor — endogenous secretion, degradation, and vagal afferent transmission — is measurably altered in T2D, and intact vagal innervation is required for native GLP-1 to suppress intake in humans; but incretin secretion is on average preserved and falls only with worsening glycaemic control, the portal afferent receptor appears not to be engaged by exogenous agonists, and prolonging native GLP-1 pharmacologically does not reduce weight. The deficit is also not specific to incretin drugs: it appears with pharmacotherapies acting on unrelated targets and after bariatric surgery. That places the mechanism in the host rather than in incretin receptor pharmacology, but it does not identify which host mechanism, because three remain compatible with all the evidence and are not mutually exclusive: the energetics of glycaemic correction; a generalised deficit in anorectic responsiveness; and degradation of the shared multi-axis gut–brain afferent pathway. Correction of hyperglycaemia removes urinary glucose energy losses and lowers resting energy expenditure; our calculations suggest these offsets could account for roughly 1–6 kg by 68–72 weeks, and an energy-balance model partially reproduces the gap from these terms alone, though it held appetite parameters fixed and so could not express the competing hypotheses. Neither component has been measured in an incretin trial. The deficit already present in prediabetes, where glycosuria is negligible, favours the appetite and afferent accounts; the persistence of the gap under enforced fixed intake favours the energetic one. Co-prescribed weight-promoting drugs, differences in age and sex, and behavioural factors probably contribute. Evidence for an intrinsic, insulin-resistance-driven resistance to incretin pharmacology is weak. The most important missing evidence is an energy-balance study, under matched conditions, that measures intake, expenditure and urinary energy losses in people with and without T2D receiving the same agent; energy intake is the measurement that discriminates between the competing accounts.

Key points

  • Across eight matched comparisons of five incretin-based molecules (same dose, duration and estimand), placebo-adjusted weight loss in type 2 diabetes was a median 0.62 times (range 0.50–0.78) that in people without diabetes: an absolute deficit of 2–7 percentage points.
  • The deficit is graded across normoglycaemia, prediabetes and diabetes, appears with GLP-1, GIP/GLP-1, amylin/GLP-1 and GIP-antagonist/GLP-1 agents, and lies in the drug-attributable component, not the placebo response.
  • Appetite suppression by incretin agonists is preserved in type 2 diabetes, but its magnitude has never been compared with that in people without diabetes.
  • Correcting hyperglycaemia returns urinary glucose energy and lowers resting energy expenditure; assumption-driven estimates indicate these drug-arm-specific offsets could account for about 1–6 kg by 68–72 weeks, but neither has been measured in an incretin trial.
  • Impaired GIP signalling or intrinsic insulin-resistance-driven pharmacological resistance are the least supported explanations; a matched energy-balance study is the key missing experiment.

1. Introduction

Medicines that act on the glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor were first developed to lower glucose. They amplify glucose-dependent insulin secretion and suppress glucagon. They later proved to be the most effective drug treatments for obesity, and newer molecules add activity at the glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP), glucagon or amylin receptors1,2. The same drugs are therefore used for two related but distinct goals: correcting dysglycaemia and reducing adiposity.

A consistent observation accompanies this dual use. When one molecule is studied at the same dose and for the same duration in separate trials of people with and without T2D, the trials in T2D report smaller weight reductions. For semaglutide 2.4 mg the placebo-adjusted reduction at 68 weeks was 12.4 percentage points without diabetes3 and 6.2 points with T2D4. Similar differences appear with tirzepatide5,6, with the oral small-molecule agonist orforglipron7,8 and with cagrilintide–semaglutide9,10.

The difference matters clinically, because weight reduction of this size changes glycaemic, cardiovascular and functional outcomes in T2D, and smaller responses shape both expectations and choice of agent. It is also informative mechanistically. Glucose lowering and weight reduction are separate pharmacological outputs. Correcting hyperglycaemia depends mainly on islet effects, whereas weight reduction depends on a sustained negative energy balance. The mechanisms that correct dysglycaemia need not determine weight loss, and correcting dysglycaemia can itself change energy balance.

This review addresses two questions separately. The clinical question is how confidently we can conclude that T2D is associated with less weight loss on comparable incretin-based therapy, and how large the difference is. The mechanistic question is which physiological or pharmacological processes could explain the difference, and which have actually been shown to do so. We consider four classes of explanation: (1) direct modification by T2D of the response to receptor agonism, whether centrally or through the peripheral secretory, degradative and afferent machinery that surrounds the receptor; (2) indirect metabolic effects of glycaemic correction on energy balance; (3) clinical and behavioural factors, including co-prescribed medicines; and (4) methodological artefacts arising from comparisons between trials. Throughout, we distinguish established causal links, supportive human evidence, indirect inference and hypotheses.

The evidence was assembled through structured searches of PubMed/MEDLINE and ClinicalTrials.gov, with backward citation tracking from pivotal trials. Every quantitative claim was checked against the primary abstract or, where accessible, the full text. This is a narrative review; it is not a systematic review and makes no claim of exhaustive screening (see Supplementary Methods and the quality-control report).

2. Clinical evidence: is weight loss attenuated in T2D?

2.1 What kind of comparison can answer the question?

Three kinds of evidence bear on the question, and they differ in strength. The strongest would be a randomised trial that stratifies by diabetes status and formally tests whether the treatment effect differs between strata (effect modification). No such trial has been designed for weight as its primary question. The next best are comparisons in which one protocol, or harmonised protocols, enrolled both populations, ideally analysed together from individual participant data (IPD). Weakest, but most plentiful, are comparisons of separate trials of the same molecule, dose, duration and estimand within one development programme. Comparisons of mean weight change between unrelated trials cannot distinguish an effect of diabetes from differences in population, background therapy or trial conduct. They also cannot show that individual patients respond differently, as opposed to populations differing on average.

2.2 Matched programmes

For semaglutide 2.4 mg weekly over 68 weeks, STEP 1 (no diabetes; baseline weight 105.3 kg, BMI 37.9 kg/m², HbA1c 5.7%) reported −14.9% versus −2.4% with placebo, an estimated treatment difference (ETD) of −12.4 percentage points3. STEP 2 (T2D; HbA1c 7–10%) reported −9.6% versus −3.4%, an ETD of −6.2 points4. The same pattern recurs at a higher dose in a later pair of trials from the same programme. STEP UP (no diabetes, n=1407; baseline weight 113.0 kg, BMI 39.9 kg/m², 73.7% women) reported −18.7% with semaglutide 7.2 mg versus −3.9% with placebo, an ETD of −14.8 points11. STEP UP T2D (n=512; baseline weight 110.1 kg, BMI 38.6 kg/m², HbA1c 8.1%, 51.8% women) reported −13.2% versus −3.9%, an ETD of −9.3 points12. This is the cleanest matched pair available: same sponsor, dose, duration and estimand, and an identical placebo-arm weight change of −3.9% in both populations. For tirzepatide over 72 weeks, SURMOUNT-1 (no diabetes) reported −19.5% at 10 mg and −20.9% at 15 mg versus −3.1% with placebo5. SURMOUNT-2 (T2D; mean HbA1c 8.02%, diabetes duration 8.5 years) reported −12.8% and −14.7% versus −3.2%6. For orforglipron 36 mg over 72 weeks the results were −11.2% versus −2.1% without diabetes7 and −9.6% versus −2.5% with T2D8. For cagrilintide–semaglutide over 68 weeks they were −20.4% versus −3.0% (ETD −17.3)9 and −13.7% versus −3.4% (ETD −10.4)10. Liraglutide 3.0 mg showed the same direction: a placebo-adjusted −5.6 kg without diabetes13 and −4.2 kg with T2D14, from similar baseline weights (about 106 kg).

Several other programmes point the same way but cannot be matched as cleanly. With retatrutide, a GIP/GLP-1/glucagon receptor tri-agonist, phase 2 weight loss reached 24.2% at 48 weeks in obesity15 and 16.9% at 36 weeks in T2D16. The durations differ, so the comparison is only qualitative. Oral semaglutide 50 mg produced a 15.1% reduction (ETD −12.7) in people without diabetes17. The corresponding T2D trial of 25 and 50 mg had no placebo arm18. In the SURPASS and SUSTAIN programmes in T2D, doses were chosen for glycaemic control and comparators were active. Weight reductions were 7.0–9.5 kg with tirzepatide monotherapy over 40 weeks19, 1.9–5.5 kg greater with tirzepatide than with semaglutide 1 mg20, and only 0.9 kg greater with semaglutide 2.0 mg than with 1.0 mg21. These T2D trials show that weight response rises with dose and potency in diabetes. They do not provide a matched comparator without diabetes.

Across the nine percentage-based comparisons, the placebo-adjusted reduction in T2D was a median 0.63 times that without diabetes (range 0.50–0.78). The absolute deficit was a median 5.5 percentage points (range 1.8–6.9) (Table 1; analysis/derived.py). Two features stand out. First, the ratio is broadly similar for selective GLP-1 receptor agonists, the GIP/GLP-1 co-agonist tirzepatide and the amylin–GLP-1 combination. Second, placebo-arm weight change was not smaller in T2D (−3.4% vs −2.4% for semaglutide 2.4 mg; −3.9% vs −3.9% for semaglutide 7.2 mg; −3.2% vs −3.1% for tirzepatide; −2.5% vs −2.1% for orforglipron; −3.4% vs −3.0% for cagrilintide–semaglutide). The deficit therefore lies in the drug-attributable component rather than in the response to the lifestyle programme delivered to both arms.

2.3 Harmonised and single-protocol evidence

The STEP-HFpEF programme provides the closest available approach to a direct test. Two trials with harmonised eligibility criteria, endpoints and analyses enrolled people with obesity-related heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, one without diabetes and one with T2D. Both gave semaglutide 2.4 mg for 52 weeks. Weight change was −13.3% versus −2.6% without diabetes (ETD −10.7)22 and −9.8% versus −3.4% with T2D (ETD −6.4)23. The prespecified IPD pooled analysis found substantial heterogeneity between the trials for weight (p<0.0001; I² 95.8%), whereas improvements in heart-failure symptoms were consistent24. The authors note roughly 40% less weight loss with diabetes. Even here, diabetes status was not randomised, and the trials differed in recruitment and background therapy.

The phase 2 trial of maridebart cafraglutide, which combines GLP-1 receptor agonism with GIP receptor antagonism, enrolled an obesity cohort and an obesity-with-T2D cohort under one protocol25. At 52 weeks, weight change ranged from −12.3% to −16.2% (placebo −2.5%) in the obesity cohort and from −8.4% to −12.3% (placebo −1.7%) in the T2D cohort. The dosing arms were not identical between cohorts, and the T2D cohort was older (55.1 vs 47.9 years) and included fewer women (42% vs 63%).

Observational data point the same way. In a referral-centre cohort treated with semaglutide 1.7–2.4 mg, people with T2D lost 7.2% versus 11.8% at 6 months26. In a large propensity-matched electronic-record cohort, people without T2D lost more weight than people with T2D on both tirzepatide and semaglutide. The authors explicitly raise differences in motivation and concurrent weight-loss activity as possible explanations27.

2.4 A gradient with dysglycaemia

The gradient also appears within T2D, indexed by glycaemic control rather than by diabetes status. In SURMOUNT-2, weight reduction with tirzepatide varied meaningfully by baseline HbA1c category28, and pooled patient-level analyses of the SUSTAIN programme found baseline characteristics including glycaemia to modify the weight response to semaglutide29. The direction is consistent: higher baseline HbA1c, smaller weight reduction. This within-disease gradient is the single most informative feature of the phenotype, because it is the common prediction of every mechanism that scales with glycaemia — the energetic offsets of Section 9 above all — and it is not predicted by an explanation resting on diabetes status as a category.

In a post hoc analysis of STEP 1, participants with prediabetes lost 13.7% with semaglutide (ETD −11.3)30, compared with 14.9% in the whole semaglutide arm3. Assuming the overall mean is the weighted average of the subgroup means, normoglycaemic participants lost about 15.9%. Normoglycaemia (~15.9%), prediabetes (13.7%) and T2D (9.6%) therefore form a graded series on the same dose and duration (Figure 1B). The gradient is unadjusted and comes from different trials, so it does not establish dose–response causation. It does argue against a threshold effect confined to overt diabetes. Its presence in prediabetes, where glycosuria is negligible, also limits how much of the gap any glycosuria-based explanation alone can carry (Section 9). In SURMOUNT-1, participants with prediabetes still lost 18.7–19.7% with tirzepatide 10–15 mg over 176 weeks31, so large responses remain achievable in dysglycaemia.

2.5 The gap is not specific to incretin drugs

A phenotype confined to incretin pharmacology would point to the receptor. A phenotype that appears whenever weight is lost by any means points to the host. The evidence favours the second, and this bears directly on which mechanisms in Sections 5 to 10 can be primary.

Smaller weight reductions in T2D have been reported with three non-incretin pharmacotherapies acting on entirely unrelated targets. Naltrexone–bupropion produced −5.0% versus −1.8% with placebo in T2D (ETD −3.2)32, against larger reductions in the non-diabetes COR programme33. Lorcaserin, a 5-HT2C agonist, produced −4.5% to −5.0% versus −1.5% in BLOOM-DM (ETD about −3.0)34, again below the non-diabetes BLOOM result35. Phentermine–topiramate shows the same direction36. Bariatric surgery likewise: preoperative diabetes is a recognised predictor of smaller long-term weight loss37. None of these acts on the GLP-1 or GIP receptor, and surgery is not a drug at all.

Dietary restriction is the informative exception, and it cuts both ways. In a 36-day metabolic-ward study at a fixed 300 kcal/day, participants with T2D lost 308 ± 19 g/day versus 450 ± 31 g/day in non-diabetic participants (p<0.01) — but the non-diabetic group began with significantly greater body weight and lean mass, which the authors note as a confounder38. Behavioural programmes showed the same direction39. Against this, a systematic review and meta-analysis of five low-energy formula-diet studies (n=569) found no significant difference in final weight loss between people with and without T2D: −1.2 kg (95% CI −4.1 to 1.6), with near-identical rates of loss (0.6 vs 0.5 kg/week)40.

Two readings of that null are possible. It may mean the gap is specific to pharmacotherapy and surgery and absent under enforced energy restriction. Or the meta-analysis may be underpowered and dominated by individual studies, as has been argued41. We cannot settle this from the published data.

What generality does and does not license. The inference this evidence supports is narrower than it first appears, and it is worth stating precisely because it is easy to overdraw. That the gap appears across drug classes sharing no receptor, and after surgery, makes a defect specific to incretin receptor pharmacology an unlikely primary cause. It does not identify which mechanism is responsible. It licenses only the conclusion that the operative mechanism lies in the host rather than in the drug — and at least three host-level classes of mechanism are compatible with everything above:

  1. Energetics of glycaemic correction (Section 9). Any intervention that lowers weight in T2D also corrects glycaemia, so the glycosuric and resting-expenditure offsets apply regardless of how weight loss was achieved.
  2. A generalised deficit in anorectic responsiveness (Section 4.3). Naltrexone–bupropion, lorcaserin and phentermine–topiramate all act centrally on appetite. If the diabetic state blunts the brain’s response to satiation signals in general, every one of them underperforms, and so does any incretin agent.
  3. Degraded shared downstream machinery (Section 5). All of these interventions, bariatric surgery included, ultimately route through common gut–brain afferent and hindbrain–hypothalamic pathways. Damage to that shared path blunts whatever is fed into it, whichever receptor opens the door.

These three are not mutually exclusive, and cross-intervention generality cannot distinguish among them, because all three predict it. Section 11.1 sets out the observations that would. Note also that bariatric surgery, which we cited above as evidence against a receptor-level cause, works substantially through gut-hormone remodelling — large postoperative rises in GLP-1 and PYY. If T2D has degraded the apparatus that receives those signals, surgery underperforms for reasons that have nothing to do with energetics. The surgical finding is therefore neutral between hypotheses 1 and 3, not supportive of 1.

The Henry study is the one piece of evidence here that does discriminate, and it points toward energetics: intake was fixed by protocol, so a difference in appetite could not express itself, yet the diabetic participants still lost less — while their fasting glucose fell from 291 to 95 mg/dL, closing a large glycosuric leak (Section 9). Its confounder (greater baseline weight and lean mass in the non-diabetic group) and the opposing meta-analytic null40 prevent it from settling the matter, but it is the right kind of experiment42.

2.6 Could the difference be an artefact?

Several features of the trials argue against a purely methodological explanation.

  • Dose, duration and estimand were matched within each programme.
  • Discontinuation and tolerability do not favour the trials without diabetes. Gastrointestinal adverse events were reported by 72.5% in REDEFINE 2 versus 79.6% in REDEFINE 19,10. Discontinuations for adverse events were similar in ATTAIN-1 and ATTAIN-2 (5.3–10.3% vs 6.1–9.9%)7,8.
  • Analyses that remove discontinuation leave the difference in place. Under the efficacy estimand, which estimates the effect had treatment continued as intended, SURMOUNT-2 reported ETDs of −10.1 and −12.4 points6. These remain well below the treatment-regimen ETDs of −16.4 and −17.8 points in SURMOUNT-15, although the treatment-regimen estimand includes the effect of discontinuation and so understates the drug’s full on-treatment effect.
  • Drug exposure is unlikely to explain the difference. Within T2D programmes, population pharmacokinetic analyses identify body weight as the only clinically relevant covariate of semaglutide exposure43, and weight and HbA1c responses track plasma exposure44. Baseline body weights in the matched programmes were similar. An exposure–response model of semaglutide spanning populations with and without T2D used baseline HbA1c as a covariate45, but we could not verify from the accessible text how much it changed the predicted response.

Other differences do favour the trials without diabetes. The T2D trials enrol older participants with a higher proportion of men. Women lost more weight in the STEP-HFpEF pool24; STEP 1 enrolled 74.1% women and SURMOUNT-2 50.7%3,6. T2D participants also continued glucose-lowering drugs, including sulfonylureas in 26.7% of SURMOUNT-2 participants6. No analysis has pooled IPD across programmes with adjustment for age, sex, baseline BMI, HbA1c, diabetes duration and background therapy. Without one, the share of the gap attributable to these population differences cannot be quantified.

2.7 What the clinical evidence establishes

The clinical phenotype is robust. It appears in every matched programme, in harmonised IPD, in a single-protocol trial and in observational cohorts, and it is graded with glycaemic status. On average, people with T2D achieve about 50–80% (median ~60%) of the placebo-adjusted weight reduction seen without diabetes. The absolute shortfall is about 2–7 percentage points at 52–72 weeks, larger with the most potent agents. Because the deficit occurs in the drug-attributable component, and across GLP-1 receptor agonists, GIP receptor agonism and antagonism, amylin co-agonism and lifestyle intervention39, it is more likely to reflect host physiology and context than a defect specific to one receptor. What the evidence cannot yet establish is how much of the population difference is effect modification by diabetes itself rather than confounding by co-varying traits.

3. Incretin pharmacology: separating glucose lowering from weight loss

3.1 Endogenous physiology versus pharmacology

Endogenous GLP-1 and GIP are short-lived peptides secreted after meals. Both potentiate glucose-stimulated insulin secretion. GLP-1 also suppresses glucagon, slows gastric emptying and reduces food intake1. In T2D the incretin effect, the extra insulin secreted after oral compared with intravenous glucose at matched glycaemia, is markedly reduced: incretin factors accounted for 36% of the insulin response in T2D versus 73% in matched controls46. The defect is largely a consequence of the diabetic state rather than its cause. It appears in diabetes secondary to chronic pancreatitis but not in pancreatitis with normal glucose tolerance47. Four weeks of near-normoglycaemia improved the late-phase β-cell response to GLP-1 and GIP three- to fourfold48.

These findings concern the endocrine pancreas at physiological hormone concentrations. They do not imply reduced responsiveness to long-acting agonists at pharmacological exposures. Infused GLP-1 retained most of its insulinotropic activity in mild T2D, whereas the insulinotropic effect of GIP was reduced by 54%49. GIP also failed to amplify late-phase secretion50. Graded GLP-1 infusions increased β-cell glucose responsiveness in T2D, but at each dose the response was 3–5 times smaller than in controls. Even so, a low dose restored responsiveness to the level of untreated controls51. A reduced physiological incretin effect is therefore a statement about the islet. It says nothing direct about the brain and gut circuits through which agonists reduce energy intake.

3.2 Two outputs, partly separate pathways

The glucose-lowering effects of incretin drugs arise mainly from islet mechanisms (insulin secretion and glucagon suppression), from slower gastric emptying of meal glucose, and secondarily from weight reduction and improved insulin sensitivity. In a mechanistic study in T2D, tirzepatide improved the clamp disposition index, total insulin secretion, insulin sensitivity (M value) and glucagon suppression more than semaglutide 1 mg52. Weight reduction, by contrast, depends on a sustained negative energy balance. That balance is driven mainly by reduced energy intake, with little or no increase in energy expenditure in humans (Sections 4 and 10). Both outputs share the receptor but not the effector organs. This is why a drug can improve glycaemia without changing weight, as low-dose liraglutide (0.6 mg) did in an 8-week respiratory-chamber study in T2D53, and why glycaemic correction can feed back on energy balance (Section 9).

3.3 Exposure, receptor engagement and molecular class

Long-acting peptide agonists achieve sustained plasma concentrations far above endogenous GLP-1. Within T2D, semaglutide exposure varies mainly with body weight43. Oral and subcutaneous formulations show nearly identical exposure–response relationships for HbA1c and body weight, which suggests that variation between individuals in GLP-1 receptor responsiveness is not a major determinant of response44. Agents differ in other ways. Tirzepatide engages the GIP receptor more than the GLP-1 receptor at clinical doses, and at the GLP-1 receptor it favours cAMP signalling over β-arrestin recruitment and causes less receptor internalisation54. Orforglipron is a non-peptide oral agonist7. Cagrilintide adds amylin receptor agonism9. Maridebart cafraglutide pairs GLP-1 agonism with GIP receptor antagonism25. Despite these differences, the attenuation ratio in T2D is similar across agents (Table 1). That similarity is the first indication that the explanation lies mainly in the host rather than in a receptor-specific pharmacodynamic defect.

4. Central regulation of appetite and energy intake

4.1 How incretin agonists reduce intake

Reduced energy intake is the dominant driver of incretin-induced weight loss. In adults with obesity, semaglutide 1.0 mg reduced ad libitum energy intake across a test day by 24%, with less hunger and food craving, better control of eating and a lower preference for high-fat foods. Resting metabolic rate adjusted for lean mass did not change55. Semaglutide 2.4 mg reduced ad libitum lunch intake by 35% after 20 weeks56. Tirzepatide reduced intake at an ad libitum lunch by about 525 kcal at week 3 in adults without diabetes. It also reduced food cravings, the tendency to overeat and responsiveness to food cues, and reduced activation to high-fat, high-sugar food images in orbitofrontal, cingulate and hippocampal regions57. In humans, GLP-1 receptors have been identified on neurons of the hypothalamus, medulla and parietal cortex58.

Animal studies define the circuitry. Peripherally administered semaglutide does not cross the blood–brain barrier. It reaches circumventricular organs and periventricular sites, activates hindbrain and hypothalamic neurons, and engages secondary nodes such as the lateral parabrachial nucleus. In rodents it reduces intake without lowering energy expenditure59. Liraglutide directly activates arcuate POMC/CART neurons and indirectly inhibits NPY/AgRP neurons through GABAergic transmission. Its weight-lowering effect persisted after loss of GLP-1 receptors in vagal afferents, area postrema or paraventricular nucleus60. Other work shows that vagal afferent and central receptor populations both contribute, because vagal deafferentation shifts the dose–response curve61. The pharmacological effect is also separable from the endogenous brainstem preproglucagon system. Those neurons are the predominant source of GLP-1 within the brain, but their ablation left ad libitum intake, body weight and glucose tolerance unchanged in mice, affecting intake only after a prolonged fast or a liquid preload and abolishing stress-induced hypophagia — a secondary satiation circuit rather than a primary regulator62 — and they are not required for the anorexia induced by GLP-1 receptor agonists63. These rodent data describe pathways. They do not show that any pathway behaves differently in human T2D. The peripheral limb of the same circuitry, including vagal afferent transmission and the endogenous incretin background on which the drug is superimposed, is considered separately in Section 5.

4.2 Appetite suppression in T2D: present, magnitude untested

Three types of evidence should be kept apart.

Incretin agonism suppresses appetite in T2D. This is well supported. Intravenous GLP-1 reduced energy intake by 27% in T2D64. Liraglutide 1.8 mg reduced ad libitum intake by 18% after 3 weeks65. Tirzepatide and semaglutide both reduced appetite in T2D over 28 weeks66. In a crossover fMRI study, exenatide reduced food intake and food-cue responses in insula, amygdala, putamen and orbitofrontal cortex in obese people with T2D as well as in normoglycaemic obese people, and receptor blockade with exendin 9-39 largely prevented these effects67. In T2D, liraglutide reduced insula and putamen responses to food pictures after 10 days but not after 12 weeks, relative to insulin glargine68. It also reduced parietal activation to highly desirable foods58.

Whether appetite suppression is smaller in T2D is unknown. No study has compared ad libitum intake, appetite ratings or food-cue responses between people with and without diabetes receiving the same agonist under identical conditions. Apparent differences between studies, such as 27% with GLP-1 infusion in T2D versus 12% in healthy men69, reflect different infusion rates, meals and populations and cannot be interpreted as effect modification.

Whether a difference in appetite suppression explains the weight-loss gap has therefore never been tested. One observation is suggestive. In T2D, tirzepatide produced more weight loss than semaglutide 1 mg, but intake at an ad libitum lunch did not differ between the drugs. The authors concluded that measured intake did not account for the difference in weight outcome66. A single test meal is an insensitive measure of 24-hour intake, so this finding should not be overinterpreted.

4.3 Could the diabetic state blunt central responses?

This is one of the three host-level hypotheses that the cross-intervention evidence of Section 2.5 leaves open, and it deserves more weight than the absence of direct tests might suggest. Several lines of human evidence show that the diabetic brain responds abnormally to nutrient and food signals.

The most direct is a functional MRI study of nutrient sensing. Glucose ingestion produces a prolonged inhibition of hypothalamic neuronal activity in healthy people. In seven men with T2D compared with ten age-matched healthy men, that inhibition failed to occur70. The groups were matched for body mass index (27.9 vs 26.1 kg/m²), so this is a feature of the diabetic state rather than of adiposity. The authors concluded that failure of neural circuits to adapt properly to nutrient ingestion may contribute to metabolic imbalance in T2D. The study is small, men only, and from 2007, and it used oral glucose rather than a pharmacological agonist — but it is a direct demonstration in humans that central nutrient sensing is impaired in diabetes.

Converging markers point the same way. In adults with obesity, MRI markers of mediobasal hypothalamic gliosis were greater in impaired glucose tolerance and T2D than in normal glucose tolerance, independent of adiposity, and correlated with glycaemia and insulin resistance71. Brain insulin resistance in obesity and T2D alters eating behaviour and reward processing72. In obese T2D and normoglycaemic obese participants, food-cue responses in appetite and reward regions were greater than in lean participants67, and postprandial neural processing of food cues varies with body weight and with circulating insulin73. A larger baseline drive to eat, or a hypothalamus that does not register ingested nutrient, would oppose a given pharmacological suppression.

Two features make this hypothesis worth taking seriously alongside the energetic account. First, it predicts the gap for any anorectic agent, which is what Section 2.5 observes. Second, and more tellingly, the abnormalities above are present in impaired glucose tolerance as well as overt diabetes71. That matters because the weight-loss deficit is already visible in prediabetes (Section 2.4), where glycosuria is negligible and the energetic account has least to offer. The one gradient the energetic hypothesis cannot explain is precisely the one where this hypothesis is strongest.

What is still missing is the direct test. No study has compared the magnitude of agonist-induced anorexia between people with and without diabetes under matched conditions, so whether gliosis, central insulin resistance, impaired hypothalamic nutrient sensing or heightened food-cue reactivity reduces the size of the drug effect remains untested. These are well-motivated hypotheses with human mechanistic support, not established mediators.

4.4 Compensatory regulation

Weight loss provokes compensatory increases in appetite. In patients treated with canagliflozin, whose energy deficit comes from glycosuria without appetite suppression, calculated energy intake rose by about 100 kcal/day for each kilogram lost. That is more than three times the accompanying adaptation in energy expenditure74. A common interpretation, not directly tested in humans, is that incretin agonists shift the level at which this feedback operates. Under that model, any additional, drug-independent energy inflow in T2D, such as glucose energy previously lost in urine (Section 9), would act on this feedback system and lead to a higher weight plateau.

5. Peripheral incretin signalling: the endogenous background, its degradation and afferent transmission

Section 4 considered the brain. This section considers everything upstream of it: the endogenous incretin secreted by the gut, the enzymes that destroy it, the receptors it meets in the gut wall and hepatoportal bed, and the afferent nerves that carry the resulting signal. Each of these is altered in T2D. Whether any of it changes how a pharmacological agonist performs is a separate question, and the answer turns on a single issue of pharmacology that we take first.

5.1 Why the endogenous background might matter at all

A long-acting agonist does not need endogenous GLP-1 to activate the GLP-1 receptor. It reaches sustained plasma concentrations far above physiological ones (Section 3.3), and it is engineered to resist the enzymatic degradation that limits the native peptide75. On the simplest reading, the state of the endogenous system is therefore irrelevant to drug effect, and reduced native secretion in T2D would predict a larger relative response to an agonist, not a smaller one.

Two considerations complicate that reading. First, endogenous GLP-1 acts substantially where it is released. Native GLP-1 has a circulating half-life of minutes, and a large fraction of an intravenous bolus is inactivated before it leaves the portal circulation76,77. Much of its physiological action is therefore paracrine and neural, exerted on receptors in the gut wall and hepatoportal bed before the peptide reaches the systemic circulation at all76. A drug delivered subcutaneously arrives at those same receptors from the systemic side, at different concentrations and without the postprandial timing. Second, the pathway that carries the signal onward — vagal afferent fibres and their brainstem targets — is itself a structure that diabetes damages (Section 5.5). If the drug’s effect depends partly on that pathway, then degrading the pathway could degrade the drug effect regardless of receptor occupancy.

The peripheral hypothesis, then, is not that T2D leaves too little endogenous GLP-1 for the drug to work with. It is that T2D may erode a paracrine and neural substrate on which part of the agonist’s anorectic effect depends, and may remove a physiological signal that normally works alongside it.

5.2 Endogenous GLP-1 and GIP secretion in T2D: contested, and graded with glycaemia

Individual studies support reduced GLP-1 secretion in T2D. In 54 patients with T2D compared with 33 controls with normal glucose tolerance, the 4-hour meal GLP-1 response was lower (area under the curve 2482 ± 145 versus 3101 ± 198 pmol/L × 240 min; p = 0.024), and a model including diabetes, BMI, male sex and other terms explained 42% of the variance in that response78. In a meal study of eight obese patients with T2D against eight matched obese healthy participants, GLP-1 responses were reduced in T2D while GIP responses were normal79.

Systematic evidence qualifies this substantially. A meta-analysis of 22 trials and 29 stimulation tests (275 patients with T2D, 279 controls) found no significant difference in total GLP-1 responses by any measure — peak concentration, total or incremental area under the curve, or their time-corrected equivalents80. A companion meta-analysis of 23 trials found GIP secretion likewise preserved81. The authors concluded that patients with T2D do not in general show reduced incretin secretion.

The qualification that survives is the one that matters most here. In both meta-analyses, meta-regression showed that HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose predicted the incremental GLP-1 response, and HbA1c predicted GIP responses negatively80,81. Reduced secretion is thus not a property of having diabetes but a function of how poorly controlled it is. This matters for two reasons. It means a uniform secretory deficit cannot be assumed in trial populations, whose mean HbA1c was around 8% (Table 1). And it predicts a graded rather than threshold relationship, which is the shape the clinical phenotype actually has (Section 2.4). The same logic applies at the islet: near-normalisation of glycaemia for four weeks improved the β-cell response to both GLP-1 and GIP three- to fourfold48, and in rat islets chronic hyperglycaemia reduced GLP-1 and GIP receptor expression reversibly82.

5.3 Degradation: DPP-4 and neprilysin

Dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) cleaves GLP-1(7–36) amide to an inactive form and is expressed on endothelium throughout the portal and systemic circulation76. Higher DPP-4 activity would lower the active fraction of endogenous GLP-1 reaching paracrine and portal receptors — a second reduction compounding any secretory deficit.

The human evidence is weaker than it is often presented. In 985 people with newly diagnosed T2D, plasma DPP-4 activity correlated positively with HbA1c and HOMA-IR, and those in the highest activity quartile had worse metabolic profiles83. This is a cross-sectional association within T2D; it does not establish that activity is higher than in people without diabetes. A 12-week exercise and weight-loss intervention in 14 older adults with metabolic syndrome lowered plasma DPP-4 by 10%, and the fall correlated with improved clamp-derived insulin sensitivity (r = −0.59)84 — an intervention study with no non-diabetic comparator, n = 14, and no measurement of GLP-1 or body weight. Neither study supports a quantitative claim about how much active GLP-1 is lost.

Two mechanistic observations are nonetheless informative about where degradation matters. In mice, blood-glucose lowering after the DPP-4 inhibitor anagliptin correlated with small-intestinal DPP-4 inhibition (r = 0.949) but not with plasma DPP-4 inhibition, in both normal and diabetic models, with intact GLP-1 rising in the portal circulation85. And neprilysin, upregulated in obesity and T2D, also degrades GLP-1: high-fat-fed neprilysin-deficient mice had higher active GLP-1 (5.8 ± 1.1 versus 3.5 ± 0.8 pmol/L), lower plasma DPP-4 activity and better glucose tolerance86. Both findings locate the degradative bottleneck in the gut and portal bed rather than the systemic circulation. Both are rodent studies, and the neprilysin result is a genetic deletion, not pharmacological inhibition.

The clinical counterpoint is decisive for the drug question. DPP-4 inhibitors raise endogenous active GLP-1 and improve glycaemia, yet produce negligible weight loss. Prolonging the residence of native GLP-1 is therefore not sufficient to reduce body weight, which bounds how much any degradation-based mechanism can contribute to a weight-loss deficit.

5.4 Where peripheral GLP-1 acts: paracrine vagal afferents, not the hepatoportal route

GLP-1 receptors are expressed on vagal afferent neurons of the nodose ganglion, which project to the nucleus tractus solitarii and onward to hypothalamic feeding centres61,77. Three lines of animal work define the anatomy more precisely, and they do not all point the same way.

A portal glucose sensor exists and is GLP-1-receptor-dependent. Low-rate intragastric glucose in mice increased muscle glycogen synthesis by 30% and induced c-Fos in brainstem and hypothalamus, colocalising with arcuate NPY neurons. The response was diminished by central exendin 9-39 and abolished in GLP-1 receptor knockout mice — and, importantly, high-fat feeding prevented it entirely87. Related work implicates intestinal gluconeogenesis in generating the portal glucose signal detected by periportal afferents88. Note that the receptor dependence demonstrated in this model was central, not portal.

But the hepatoportal branch is not the required route. Selective ablation of the common hepatic branch of the vagus did not abolish the GLP-1-receptor-mediated incretin response or GLP-1’s suppression of intake in rats, whereas complete subdiaphragmatic vagal deafferentation did89. The authors concluded that what matters is paracrine signalling on GLP-1 receptors borne by vagal afferents of general gastrointestinal origin, not the hepatoportal bed specifically.

And circulating GLP-1 may not need vagal afferents at all. Intravenous GLP-1 reduced sucrose intake in rats undiminished by vagotomy or capsaicin treatment — in fact enhanced at the highest dose — with comparable hindbrain c-Fos induction in all groups90. The authors inferred that vagal participation is limited to paracrine effects near the sites of secretion, and that endocrine GLP-1 acts elsewhere. Conversely, the anorectic and glucose-tolerance effects of the GLP-1-releasing sweetener D-allulose were blunted by vagotomy, GLP-1 receptor blockade and vagal-afferent-selective GLP-1 receptor inactivation91, consistent with a vagal route for locally released GLP-1.

The most important finding for the present argument concerns receptor identity. In anaesthetised rats, intraportal GLP-1(7–36) amide at both periphysiological (0.2 pmol) and pharmacological (4.0 pmol) doses increased hepatic vagal afferent discharge. Exendin-4 did not, at any dose up to 40 pmol, and exendin 9-39 at more than 100-fold molar excess failed to block the response to GLP-1(7–36) amide92. The hepatic vagal reception of portal GLP-1 therefore appears to operate through a receptor pharmacologically distinct from the classical GLP-1 receptor — one that a GLP-1 receptor agonist does not engage. Consistent with this, intraportal exenatide in conscious dogs produced no insulin-independent effect on hepatic glucose metabolism93.

This is a problem for the peripheral hypothesis, not a support for it, and it should be stated plainly. If the portal afferent pathway is not activated by exogenous agonists, then loss of endogenous portal signalling in T2D removes a physiological contribution that the drug was never supplying, in both populations. It would not blunt the drug’s effect; it would lower the baseline on which the drug acts. The receptor in question has also never been molecularly identified, the experiments are from 2000 in small numbers of anaesthetised rats, and no one has repeated them with a modern long-acting agonist. The claim should be treated as unresolved rather than established.

5.5 Human evidence that intact vagal innervation matters

One human study bears directly on whether peripheral neural transmission is necessary for GLP-1’s effect on eating. Twenty men with truncal vagotomy and pyloroplasty and ten matched healthy controls received GLP-1(7–36) amide or saline during a standardised meal followed by an ad libitum meal. GLP-1 significantly reduced ad libitum food intake in controls but had no effect in the vagotomised group. It delayed gastric emptying in controls but not after vagotomy, and left insulin secretion unaffected in the vagotomised group while suppressing it in controls. Postprandial glucose fell by the same percentage in both groups, and peak postprandial GLP-1 concentrations were roughly fivefold higher after vagotomy94.

This is the strongest available human evidence that intact vagal innervation is required for exogenous GLP-1 to reduce food intake. Its limitations are material. The participants were men with surgical vagotomy and pyloroplasty for ulcer disease, a far more complete and anatomically different lesion than diabetic autonomic neuropathy; pyloroplasty itself accelerates gastric emptying and confounds the intake measurement; the infusion delivered native GLP-1, not a long-acting agonist engineered to act systemically; and the study was not designed to measure body weight over time. It establishes that the vagal route can be rate-limiting for native GLP-1 in humans. It does not establish that partial vagal damage limits the effect of semaglutide or tirzepatide.

5.6 Vagal and enteric neuropathy in diabetes

Autonomic neuropathy is common in T2D and begins early. In 806 patients with newly diagnosed T2D undergoing systematic assessment, cardiovascular autonomic neuropathy was present in 18.6% and somatic neuropathy in 21.2%95. Reported prevalence across populations and diagnostic methods ranges very widely, from a few per cent to the large majority, depending on the tests applied and the cohort studied96. Cardiovascular autonomic function is a proxy for vagal integrity, not a measure of the gastrointestinal afferent limb.

Direct evidence that the afferent limb is affected is largely preclinical or from type 1 diabetes. A post-mortem study found severe loss of myelinated fibres in the vagus nerve and sympathetic trunks, with ganglion cell changes and inflammatory infiltrates — but in five cases of early-onset insulin-dependent diabetes with established clinical autonomic failure97, which limits its transfer to typical T2D trial populations. In db/db mice, morphometric fibre loss and shrinkage appeared across peripheral nerves, though changes in the vagus appeared late relative to other nerves98.

Two animal studies address function in type 2 models specifically, and they are the most relevant of this group. In UCD-T2DM rats, orogastric glucose activated duodenal enterochromaffin, K and L cells, enteric neurons and vagal afferent neurons in prediabetic animals, but this activation was markedly reduced in recent-onset and 3-month diabetic animals, with reduced GLP-1 immunoreactivity (and preserved GIP)99. In insulin-resistant obese animals studied with a gallium-labelled GLP-1 receptor PET probe and single-neuron electrophysiology, portal vagal afferents failed to modulate firing during glucose infusion, alongside reduced GLP-1 receptor binding potential, most marked between the splenic vein and the hepatic inlet100. Both show that the gut–vagal glucose-sensing pathway degrades with the diabetic and insulin-resistant state. Neither administered a GLP-1 receptor agonist or measured body weight.

5.7 The one study that tests the drug question — and what it does and does not show

Only one study we identified blunted an agonist’s effect alongside peripheral pathway changes. In obese-prone and obese-resistant rats, intraperitoneal exendin-4 suppressed intake effectively in both strains on chow. On a high-energy, high-fat diet, obese-prone rats suppressed intake significantly less than obese-resistant rats at every dose tested. This was accompanied by downregulated GLP-1 receptor mRNA in the vagal nodose ganglia, lower plasma GLP-1, reduced intestinal GLP-1 protein and a reduced number of L cells in the distal ileum101.

This is the closest existing analogue of the clinical observation: a dysmetabolic host showing reduced anorectic response to a GLP-1 receptor agonist, with a peripheral mechanism measured in the same animals. It is also a diet-induced obesity model rather than a diabetes model, the agonist is short-acting and given intraperitoneally, the endpoint is acute intake rather than sustained weight change, and the design cannot separate nodose receptor downregulation from the several other changes present.

5.8 What the peripheral axis can and cannot carry

Taken together, the peripheral evidence supports a coherent hypothesis and falls well short of establishing it.

In favour: the gut–vagal afferent pathway is measurably degraded in type 2 diabetic and insulin-resistant animals99,100; intact vagal innervation is required for native GLP-1 to reduce intake in humans94; agonist-induced anorexia is blunted in a dysmetabolic rodent host with nodose GLP-1 receptor downregulation101; and secretory and islet responses recover when glycaemia is corrected48,80, which fits the graded clinical phenotype.

Against, or limiting: incretin secretion is on average preserved in T2D, with reduction confined to poorer glycaemic control80,81; the portal afferent receptor appears not to be engaged by agonists at all92,93; the hepatoportal branch is not the required route even for native GLP-189; circulating GLP-1 can suppress intake without vagal afferents90; central GLP-1 receptor populations are sufficient for agonist-induced anorexia in rodents59,60; and prolonging native GLP-1 with DPP-4 inhibition does not produce weight loss.

Three further arguments bound the contribution — but one apparent bound is not one, and it is worth separating them carefully.

The cross-intervention evidence does not bound this hypothesis; it depends on which version is meant. The gap appears with naltrexone–bupropion, lorcaserin and phentermine–topiramate, none of which touches the GLP-1 or GIP receptor, and after bariatric surgery (Section 2.5)32,34,36,37. This does rule out the narrow version of the peripheral hypothesis — that reduced endogenous GLP-1, raised DPP-4 activity or lost portal GLP-1 signalling is the primary cause — because none of those is engaged when no incretin drug is given.

It does not touch the broad version, and arguably supports it. The vagal afferent pathway described in Sections 5.4 to 5.6 is not an incretin-specific channel. The same fibres carry cholecystokinin, PYY, ghrelin, distension and nutrient signals, and the same hindbrain and hypothalamic nodes integrate them77,102. A degraded afferent path blunts whatever is fed into it. That predicts a deficit with every anorectic agent, and with bariatric surgery — which works substantially through large postoperative rises in GLP-1 and PYY, and therefore depends on an intact receiving apparatus. The generality of the phenotype is thus as consistent with damage to shared gut–brain machinery as with the energetics of glycaemic correction. Our earlier framing of this evidence as a bound on the present section was mistaken; it bounds only the incretin-specific reading.

The honest state of this literature, however, is that the multi-axis version has not been tested either. A comprehensive review of ghrelin, CCK, GLP-1 and PYY concludes that while CCK is established as an endogenous endocrine control of eating in healthy-weight people, the roles of all four hormones in eating in obesity and after bypass surgery remain uncertain, with local rather than endocrine signalling likely and no good method for assessing it102. One T2D-specific study illustrates how interconnected these axes are: in metformin-treated patients with T2D, cholecystokinin-induced gallbladder emptying stimulated GLP-1 secretion through bile acid release, and a single dose of the bile acid sequestrant sevelamer abolished that stimulation103. Bile acid, CCK and L-cell signalling are one system, not three, and diabetes perturbs all of it.

Of the genuine bounds, the first is that the cross-agent test used in Section 7.3 applies here too. If the deficit depended on the endogenous GLP-1 background or its degradation, it should be smaller for agents that bypass both. Orforglipron is a non-peptide small molecule that is not a DPP-4 substrate, and cagrilintide–semaglutide adds amylin receptor agonism; both show the deficit (ratios 0.71–0.78 and 0.60; Table 1). The attenuation is not confined to agents whose action depends on the native incretin axis. Second, almost all participants in the T2D trials were taking metformin — 89% in SURMOUNT-26 — and metformin itself raises GLP-1 concentrations and alters the intestinal bile acid pool and microbiome104. The endogenous background in these populations is pharmacologically modified in the direction opposite to the hypothesised deficit. Third, drug exposure is not reduced in T2D: oral semaglutide bioavailability did not differ significantly between healthy participants and people with T2D, and subcutaneous exposure varies mainly with body weight43,105.

The honest position is that the peripheral axis is a well-motivated, mechanistically detailed and almost entirely untested contributor. Its strongest form is not that agonists work less well on diabetic receptors — that version is weak and partly refuted. It is that T2D degrades a shared, multi-axis afferent pathway through which satiation signals of every kind must pass, removing a physiological component that normally operates alongside the drug, and that this loss is graded with glycaemic control. That proposition has never been measured in either population receiving an incretin agent. Section 12 sets out the studies that would test it, including the two most direct: whether baseline autonomic function or measured endogenous incretin secretion predicts weight response to an agonist, and whether the response differs in people with established vagal dysfunction.

6. Gastrointestinal physiology and gastric emptying

GLP-1 slows gastric emptying acutely. With sustained receptor exposure this effect diminishes. Tachyphylaxis of the delay was evident by a second meal during an 8.5-hour GLP-1 infusion, together with loss of the associated vagal marker pancreatic polypeptide106. A continuous 24-hour infusion attenuated the delay, whereas intermittent exposure preserved it107. With long-acting agents the picture is mixed. After 20 weeks of semaglutide 2.4 mg, paracetamol absorption showed no delay56. Semaglutide 1.0 mg delayed only first-hour emptying108. Exenatide once weekly still slowed scintigraphically measured emptying of solids and liquids at 8 weeks109. Liraglutide 3.0 mg delayed solid emptying at 5 and 16 weeks in obesity, and the delay at 5 weeks correlated with weight loss at 16 weeks110. Tirzepatide’s delay of gastric emptying likewise waned with repeated dosing in healthy participants. In T2D, however, a residual delay persisted during dose escalation, and a long-acting GIP analogue alone did not affect emptying111.

Diabetes affects this axis in ways that could push in either direction. Acute hyperglycaemia slows gastric emptying, as shown in type 1 diabetes under glucose clamp112. Diabetic autonomic neuropathy can cause gastroparesis113. The direction is not uniform, however, and the common assumption that diabetes means slow emptying is wrong as often as it is right. Among 108 patients with diabetes referred for upper gastrointestinal symptoms and tested by scintigraphy, emptying was abnormal in 56% but rapid in 37% and slow in only 19%; 40.3% of those with T2D had accelerated emptying at one hour114. Baseline emptying rate in a T2D trial population is therefore likely to be heterogeneous in both directions, which blunts any prediction that rests on it being uniformly delayed. The glucose-lowering effect of GLP-1 receptor agonism through slower gastric emptying depends on the baseline emptying rate115,116. If emptying is already slow in some people with T2D, the incremental slowing a drug can produce, and any satiation it adds, may be smaller. Conversely, correcting hyperglycaemia could speed emptying, partly offsetting the drug effect. Neither possibility has been tested against weight outcomes.

The weight of evidence suggests that gastric emptying contributes to early satiation and perhaps to variation between individuals in response110. It is not the principal mechanism of long-term weight loss, because weight loss continues after the delay has largely waned, and central receptor populations are sufficient for anorexia in animal models59,60. Gastrointestinal changes in T2D are therefore best regarded as a variable and probably minor modifier. They would need to be measured, together with weight, in both populations before they could be assigned any share of the gap. Tolerability, the clinically visible face of gut effects, does not appear worse in T2D trials (Section 2.6).

7. GIP biology and dual receptor agonism

7.1 Established human pharmacology

The insulinotropic action of GIP is reduced in T2D, both in peak effect49 and in amplifying late-phase secretion50. Near-normalisation of glycaemia partly restores it48. In rat islets, chronic hyperglycaemia reduces expression of both GLP-1 and GIP receptors, and normalising glucose reverses the change82. This finding suggests a mechanism, but it is animal data. GIP has adipose actions in humans. Together with hyperinsulinaemia it increased subcutaneous adipose blood flow, glucose uptake, triglyceride hydrolysis and fatty-acid re-esterification117. Infused GIP did not reduce energy intake in overweight men, and adding GIP to GLP-1 did not enhance the GLP-1-induced reduction in intake118.

7.2 Mechanisms proposed mainly from animal and cellular studies

In mice, hypothalamic cells expressing the GIP receptor suppress food intake when activated119. Central GIP receptors are required for the anorectic effect of acyl-GIP and for the greater efficacy of GLP-1/GIP co-agonism over GLP-1 alone120. In mice, rats and shrews, GIP receptor agonism attenuates GLP-1-induced emesis while preserving reduced food intake121. Paradoxically, GIP receptor antagonist antibodies also reduce weight and enhance GLP-1 agonist effects in rodents and non-human primates122. One explanation is that sustained agonism produces functional antagonism through receptor desensitisation, but this remains unresolved. At the receptor level, tirzepatide is biased in its signalling and engages the GIP receptor more than the GLP-1 receptor54.

7.3 Does altered GIP physiology in T2D predict a different response to dual agonism?

If reduced GIP receptor responsiveness in T2D blunted the extra weight loss attributable to GIP agonism, the ratio of T2D to non-T2D weight loss should be lower for tirzepatide than for selective GLP-1 receptor agonists. It is not: the ratio was 0.59–0.65 for tirzepatide and 0.50–0.60 for semaglutide (Table 1). The attenuation is also present with maridebart cafraglutide, which antagonises the GIP receptor (ratio about 0.77)25, and with orforglipron, which has no GIP activity8. The weight deficit in T2D therefore does not depend on the direction of GIP receptor pharmacology. This makes impaired GIP signalling an unlikely primary explanation.

The mechanistic data in T2D point instead to effects of tirzepatide beyond intake. In T2D, tirzepatide produced more weight and fat-mass loss than semaglutide 1 mg without a measurable difference in ad libitum intake66. It also produced larger improvements in insulin sensitivity52. In people with obesity but without diabetes, tirzepatide increased fat oxidation without changing metabolic adaptation123. Larger weight loss with dual agonism does not prove a specific GIP-mediated anorectic mechanism in humans. The relative contributions of greater GLP-1 receptor exposure, GIP-mediated tolerability, adipose effects and substrate effects are not established.

8. Insulin resistance, hyperinsulinaemia and substrate partitioning

8.1 The pathophysiological premise

Skeletal muscle is the main site of insulin-stimulated glucose disposal124. In T2D, insulin resistance affects muscle, liver and adipose tissue. Hepatic insulin resistance sustains glucose production, adipose insulin resistance weakens insulin’s suppression of lipolysis, and ectopic lipid accumulation links these defects125. Insulin-resistant muscle shows “metabolic inflexibility”: it oxidises more glucose in the fasting state but fails to switch towards glucose oxidation when stimulated by insulin126. Incretin therapy improves several of these defects. Tirzepatide increased the clamp M value in T2D52. In patients with non-alcoholic steatohepatitis, liraglutide improved hepatic and adipose insulin sensitivity and reduced de novo lipogenesis127. Semaglutide reduced HOMA-IR in people with prediabetes30.

8.2 Does insulin resistance plausibly limit weight loss?

A recurrent proposal is that insulin resistance itself, or the hyperinsulinaemia it causes, traps energy in adipose tissue or limits fat mobilisation and so slows weight loss. Three considerations argue against assigning it a primary role.

  1. Substrate handling does not set the energy balance. Muscle glucose uptake, glucose oxidation and glycogen storage describe which fuel is used and where it is stored. Total energy expenditure is set by ATP demand. A shift from glucose to fat oxidation, or the reverse, does not by itself change the energy deficit. In a controlled metabolic-ward study, isocaloric carbohydrate restriction lowered insulin and raised fat oxidation, yet produced less body-fat loss than equal-calorie fat restriction128. Insulin-driven partitioning therefore did not override energy balance.
  2. The mechanistic link is unmeasured. We found no human study in T2D that measured muscle glucose uptake, glucose oxidation, glycogen storage and total energy expenditure together during incretin treatment. The proposition that impaired insulin-mediated glucose disposal reduces incretin-associated weight loss is therefore untested.
  3. Treatment improves insulin sensitivity. Insulin sensitivity improves during treatment in both populations, which would narrow any partitioning difference over time.

One partitioning effect deserves note because it changes weight without changing fat: glycogen. Glycogen is stored with water, so a larger glycogen pool can add weight. Improved insulin action could plausibly restore muscle and liver glycogen that are depleted in poorly controlled diabetes and add weight unrelated to adiposity. This is an inference from general physiology and has not been measured in incretin trials.

8.3 Endogenous hyperinsulinaemia versus insulin and sulfonylurea therapy

Endogenous hyperinsulinaemia must be distinguished from weight-promoting medicines. In UKPDS 33, intensive treatment with insulin increased weight by 4.0 kg, and sulfonylureas by 1.7–2.6 kg, relative to conventional therapy129. In SURMOUNT-2, 26.7% of participants took sulfonylureas, and level-2 hypoglycaemia clustered in that group despite protocol-guided dose reduction6. Insulin-associated weight gain arises partly through the energetics of improved glycaemia (Section 9). Hypoglycaemia-avoidant eating is often invoked but is a hypothesis. Notably, liraglutide 3.0 mg plus intensive behavioural therapy produced a placebo-adjusted reduction of 4.3 percentage points in insulin-treated T2D130, similar to the 4.0 points in T2D without insulin14. Insulin co-therapy with down-titration therefore did not abolish the response. Background therapy is a credible contributor to the gap at population level, but no trial has stratified weight response by background agent with adequate power.

9. Glycosuria, glycaemic correction and net energy balance

9.1 The premise

When plasma glucose exceeds the renal reabsorptive capacity, glucose is lost in urine and its energy leaves the body. In people with T2D under stepped hyperglycaemic clamps, urinary glucose excretion rose from 0.06 mmol/min at 7.8 mmol/L to 0.77 mmol/min at 13.3 mmol/L. Glycosuria persisted down to 7.8 mmol/L, and the threshold varied between people from 7.7 to 12.2 mmol/L131. Restoring glycaemia closes this energy leak. The energy is then retained, acting like an increase in intake, but only in people who were glycosuric, and only in the arm whose glycaemia improves.

9.2 Measured magnitudes

Three human studies measured the energy involved directly.

  • In obese Pima Indians with T2D, 6 weeks of sulfonylurea lowered fasting glucose from 253 to 144 mg/dL and urinary glucose from 128 to 11 g/day. Resting metabolic rate per kilogram of fat-free mass also fell. To keep weight constant, daily intake had to be cut from 3070 to 2784 kcal132.
  • In type 1 diabetes, intensive insulin therapy under constant intake reduced glycosuria from 428 to 39 mmol/day. Whole-room calorimetry showed a 5% (118 kcal/day) fall in 24-hour energy expenditure, and weight increased by 2.6 kg, almost all of it fat133.
  • In insulin-treated T2D, improved glycaemia was an independent determinant of lower basal metabolic rate. The authors concluded that better glycaemic control promotes weight gain by reducing both metabolic rate and glucosuria134.

The reverse experiment confirms that urinary glucose energy matters to weight. SGLT2 inhibition with empagliflozin caused about 206 kcal/day of glycosuric loss, which predicted an 11.3 kg weight loss. Observed loss was 3.2 kg because calculated intake rose by about 13%135. With canagliflozin, intake rose by about 100 kcal/day for each kilogram lost74.

9.3 A formal model: Hall 2026

The most direct quantitative treatment of this question is a modelling study that appeared while this review was in preparation41. It is the closest existing approach to proposed study 2 in Table 4, conducted in silico rather than by measurement.

The design is the important part. A previously validated model of human energy balance dynamics136 was fitted to the semaglutide response of participants without diabetes in STEP UP11, estimating the parameters that describe how semaglutide affects appetite and energy intake. Those appetite parameters were then held fixed and the model applied to STEP UP T2D12, adding only two diabetes-specific terms:

  • Urinary glucose excretion, computed from estimated glomerular filtration rate, the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption137 and plasma glucose derived from measured HbA1c via the ADAG equations138. Mean eGFR in STEP UP T2D was 92 mL/min/1.73 m², so a normal renal threshold of 180 mg/dL was assumed.
  • Energy expenditure, reduced by 3.5 kcal/day per mg/dL fall in fasting plasma glucose, a coefficient derived from cross-sectional data in which resting energy expenditure in obese T2D was better predicted when fasting glucose was included, and in which poor glycaemic control corresponded to an REE elevation of up to 8%139.

Because appetite parameters were not refitted, any weight-loss gap the model reproduces is attributable to those two energetic terms alone, and any residual is not. The reported result is that the attenuation in patients not on background SGLT2 inhibitor therapy was partially explained by the UGE and expenditure changes41. The author’s conclusion is that these effects “can substantially offset negative energy balance,” and that they “may partially explain” the gap — not that they account for it.

The SGLT2 inhibitor prediction. The model’s most striking and counterintuitive output is that patients on stable background SGLT2 inhibitor therapy should show the greatest attenuation of weight loss. The logic is that an SGLT2 inhibitor lowers the renal threshold (modelled as 110 mg/dL), raising baseline UGE by roughly 100 g/day. Starting from that elevated baseline, semaglutide-induced glycaemic improvement produces a correspondingly larger fall in UGE, and so a larger energy offset. Crucially, the model predicts the opposite for simultaneous initiation of an SGLT2 inhibitor and an incretin agent, which should yield greater weight loss than either alone. Stable background therapy and co-initiation therefore move weight response in opposite directions — a distinction with direct implications for trial design and for interpreting the roughly 30% of STEP UP T2D participants who were on background SGLT2 inhibitors.

What the model does not settle. It is a simulation, not a measurement, and a preprint that has not been peer reviewed. The study is funded by AstraZeneca and the author is an employee of that company41; this does not invalidate the analysis, whose assumptions are stated explicitly and are independently checkable, but it should be recorded. Its own stated limitations are substantial and align closely with the gaps identified elsewhere in this review: aggregate trial means rather than individual participant data; the renal threshold estimated from the literature rather than measured; real uncertainty about the magnitude and variability of the expenditure effect; sensitivity to the choice of HbA1c-to-average-glucose equation, with DCCT-derived equations predicting substantially greater baseline UGE and hence greater attenuation; HbA1c being a lagging indicator, which may distort early trajectories; the trial not reporting results separately by background SGLT2 inhibitor status, so the two simulations were averaged 30:70; and a higher proportion of women in STEP UP than in STEP UP T2D (~74% vs ~52%), a known modifier of weight response. The author’s own recommendation is that future studies directly measure dynamic changes in UGE and energy expenditure to test the predictions.

One discrepancy should be flagged. The weight losses quoted in the preprint for STEP UP (approximately 18% and 21% at 72 weeks for 2.4 mg and 7.2 mg) and STEP UP T2D (approximately 11% and 14%) exceed the treatment-policy values in the primary publications (−15.6% and −18.7%; −13.2% for 7.2 mg)11,12. This is consistent with the model having been fitted to a trial-product or on-treatment estimand, but the preprint does not state which estimand was simulated. Table 1 uses the published treatment-policy values throughout.

9.4 Our own scenario estimates

None of the incretin trials measured urinary glucose or energy expenditure directly. Independently of the model above, we built transparent scenario estimates to bound the effect from measured inputs (Table 2; analysis/derived.py). Each assumes a differential reduction in glycosuria between drug and placebo arms, plus a differential fall in resting energy expenditure, persisting over the trial. We used 3.75 kcal per gram of glucose and the energy–weight relationship of Hall and colleagues: a permanent 100 kJ/day change yields about 1 kg at steady state, with half the change reached in about 1 year140. For trial-like T2D populations with baseline HbA1c of about 8%, in which the drug lowers HbA1c by about 1.5 percentage points more than placebo6, the retained energy could account for roughly 1 kg (low scenario) to 6–7 kg (high scenario) by 68–72 weeks. The observed placebo-adjusted gap between STEP 1 and STEP 2 is about 6 percentage points, or roughly 6 kg.

These calculations show that the energetics of glycaemic correction are quantitatively capable of explaining a meaningful share of the gap. They do not show that they do. Four caveats apply. Baseline glycosuria in trial populations was not measured and varies widely between individuals131. Behavioural compensation could be blunted by the drug’s own anorectic effect, or it could add to the offset. The anchoring studies of metabolic rate involved poorer control than in modern trials132. And the gradient seen in prediabetes, where glycosuria is negligible (Section 2.4), shows that glycosuria cannot be the whole explanation.

Our scenarios and the formal model agree on the central point and differ usefully in method. Both conclude that the energetics of glycaemic correction can account for a substantial but partial share of the gap. The model adds physiological structure our scenarios lack — a renal-threshold-based UGE calculation, an explicit expenditure coefficient, and dynamic rather than steady-state weight prediction — and it generates a testable prediction about background SGLT2 inhibition that scenario arithmetic could not reach. Neither rests on a single measurement of urinary glucose or energy expenditure made during incretin treatment, because none exists.

9.5 What the mechanism explains well, and what it does not

The mechanism fits three features of the clinical phenotype. It operates only in the arm whose glycaemia improves, so it predicts a deficit in drug-attributable rather than placebo-arm weight change, as observed — most cleanly in STEP UP and STEP UP T2D, where placebo-arm change was −3.9% in both11,12. Because people without diabetes have no glycosuria to lose, it predicts no offset in non-diabetes trials. And because the offset scales with the magnitude of glycaemic correction, it predicts a gradient with baseline HbA1c, which is what the within-T2D analyses show28,29.

It is also consistent with the gap appearing outside incretin pharmacology altogether (Section 2.5): any intervention that lowers weight and corrects glycaemia in T2D incurs the same energetic offset, whether it acts on the GLP-1 receptor, the 5-HT2C receptor, or the stomach32,34,37,38.

Two things it does not explain. The prediabetes step, where glycosuria is negligible yet roughly 2 percentage points of deficit have already appeared (Section 2.4). And the residual that the model itself leaves unexplained, which the author describes as partial explanation rather than full41. Something further is operating, and Sections 5 to 8 and 10 set out the candidates.

10. Adipose tissue, body composition and metabolic adaptation

Composition of weight lost. In a DXA substudy of SURMOUNT-1 (no diabetes), tirzepatide reduced weight by 21.3%, fat mass by 33.9% and lean mass by 10.9%. About three-quarters of the weight lost was fat in both drug and placebo groups141. In T2D, semaglutide 1.0 mg reduced fat mass by 3.4 kg and lean mass by 2.3 kg over 52 weeks in a SUSTAIN 8 substudy, but there was no placebo arm and the dose was lower142. Tirzepatide reduced liver fat and visceral and subcutaneous adipose volumes compared with insulin degludec143. Semaglutide 2.4 mg reduced visceral fat area by 40% in an East Asian population with and without T2D144. No study has compared body-composition changes between populations with and without T2D at matched dose and duration.

Body composition cannot easily explain smaller weight loss. Losing a kilogram of fat removes about 39.5 MJ of stored energy, compared with about 7.6 MJ for a kilogram of lean tissue140. If people with T2D lost a larger share of weight as lean tissue, a given energy deficit would produce more weight loss, not less. A composition difference could reduce weight loss only through fluid or glycogen shifts (Section 8.2). Those are short-term effects and have not been measured in this context.

Energy expenditure and adaptation. In humans, incretin agonists do not increase energy expenditure. Semaglutide did not change resting metabolic rate adjusted for lean mass55. Liraglutide did not change 24-hour expenditure in T2D53 and transiently reduced resting expenditure in a 26-week T2D trial145. Tirzepatide did not change metabolic adaptation in obesity but increased fat oxidation123. The GLP-1/glucagon co-agonist cotadutide produced weight loss in T2D driven mainly by reduced intake, with little change in expenditure146. One adaptation is specific to diabetes. Resting metabolic rate is about 5% higher in T2D than in equally obese people with normal glucose tolerance, and it falls as glycaemia is corrected132,134. Normalising glycaemia thus removes an energy cost unique to the diabetic state, adding to the glycosuric offset in Section 9.

Physical activity has not been measured objectively and compared between populations in incretin trials. Whether lower activity in older, more comorbid T2D populations contributes is unknown.

11. Integrative model: from diabetes pathophysiology to weight-loss response

Figure 2 organises the evidence into four interacting domains. Each causal link is labelled by the strength of its support. Table 3 summarises the evidence for each candidate mechanism.

Drug exposure and pharmacodynamics. Dose, duration and estimand were matched in the comparisons. Exposure depends mainly on body weight, and tolerability and discontinuation do not favour the trials without diabetes. Receptor pharmacology varies widely across agents that all show the deficit, including opposite directions of GIP receptor action. Reduced drug exposure or a receptor-specific defect is therefore unlikely to be a primary determinant.

Energy intake. Intake suppression is the main mechanism of incretin-induced weight loss in both populations and is preserved, at least qualitatively, in T2D. Its magnitude has never been compared between populations under matched conditions. A smaller central or gastrointestinal effect in T2D, through hypothalamic gliosis, central insulin resistance, greater food-cue reactivity or altered baseline gastric emptying, is biologically plausible but unmeasured. This is the largest unknown in the model.

Peripheral incretin signalling. Upstream of the brain sits a secretory, degradative and afferent apparatus that diabetes measurably alters (Section 5). The gut–vagal glucose-sensing pathway degrades in type 2 diabetic and insulin-resistant animals, intact vagal innervation is required for native GLP-1 to suppress intake in humans, and agonist-induced anorexia is blunted in a dysmetabolic rodent host with nodose receptor downregulation. Three findings bound the contribution: incretin secretion is on average preserved in T2D and falls only with worsening glycaemic control; the portal afferent receptor appears not to be engaged by exogenous agonists; and prolonging native GLP-1 with DPP-4 inhibition does not reduce weight. The most defensible version of this mechanism is therefore not pharmacological resistance but the loss of a physiological, paracrine satiation signal that normally acts alongside the drug — graded with glycaemic control, and never measured in either population on treatment.

Energy expenditure and substrate metabolism. Agonists do not raise expenditure, and adaptive changes appear similar in kind across populations. Correcting hyperglycaemia removes the excess resting metabolic rate seen in T2D. Changes in substrate partitioning and insulin sensitivity alter fuel selection, but there is no evidence that they set the size of the energy deficit.

Energy losses and glycaemic correction. Elimination of glycosuria returns previously lost glucose energy to the body, and correcting hyperglycaemia removes the excess resting metabolic rate of the diabetic state. The size of both effects is established in diabetes generally. A formal energy-balance model, fitted to non-diabetic semaglutide response and then applied to T2D with appetite parameters held fixed, partially reproduces the observed gap from these two terms alone, and predicts that stable background SGLT2 inhibition should produce the greatest attenuation41. Our independent scenario arithmetic reaches a compatible magnitude. This is now the best-supported mechanism in the model. Neither component has been measured in an incretin trial.

How several modest effects combine. Together, the energy offsets from glycaemic correction (glycosuria and metabolic rate) and weight-promoting co-therapies could narrow the net energy deficit in the drug arm. Because weight approaches a new steady state over a year or more, small persistent offsets matter: each 24 kcal/day of persistent offset corresponds to about 1 kg at steady state140. Population differences in sex and age, and possibly behaviour27,39, add further differences between trials. We deliberately do not assign percentage contributions. None of these components has been measured in both populations under the same treatment, so any apportionment would be speculative.

11.1 Three host-level hypotheses, and what would separate them

The cross-intervention evidence (Section 2.5) establishes that the operative mechanism is in the host, not in incretin receptor pharmacology. It does not say which host mechanism. Three remain live, they are not mutually exclusive, and the phenotype as currently described is compatible with all three:

  • H1 — Energetics. Correcting hyperglycaemia returns glycosuric energy and removes the excess resting metabolic rate of the diabetic state, narrowing the net deficit in the drug arm (Section 9).
  • H2 — Generalised anorectic-responsiveness deficit. The diabetic brain responds less to satiation signalling of any kind, so a given pharmacological or surgical stimulus produces less intake suppression (Section 4.3).
  • H3 — Degraded shared afferent machinery. The multi-axis gut–brain pathway that carries CCK, PYY, ghrelin, distension and incretin signals alike is damaged, blunting transmission regardless of which signal is amplified (Section 5).

One methodological point deserves emphasis because it is easy to misread. The modelling analysis of Section 9.3 held the appetite parameters fixed at their non-diabetic values41. That is what makes its energetic attribution clean, but it also means the model was never allowed to express H2. A blunted appetite response in T2D could not appear as a fitted parameter; it could only appear as residual unexplained gap — which is exactly what the analysis reports. The model therefore demonstrates that energetics can account for part of the gap. It cannot distinguish “energetics explains part and nothing else is needed” from “energetics explains part and blunted anorectic response explains the rest,” because the second possibility was excluded by construction. Refitting the appetite parameters in T2D and reporting how much they must change would be a direct and inexpensive test.

The single most discriminating measurement is therefore energy intake. If placebo-adjusted intake suppression is equal in both populations on the same agent and the weight gap persists, H1 is right and H2 is wrong. If intake suppression is smaller in T2D, H2 or H3 is operating and energetics alone cannot be the answer. No study has made this comparison (Section 4.2), which is why it is the first study in Table 4.

Which explanations are best supported? Ranked by evidence:

  • Best supported (the phenotype itself). The gap exists, is graded with glycaemic status, is confined to the drug-attributable component, and is not specific to incretin drugs — it appears with unrelated pharmacotherapies and after bariatric surgery. Drug exposure and tolerability do not explain it. The mechanism is therefore in the host.
  • Best quantified, and the only one with a formal model: energetics (H1). Glycosuric energy retention and the fall in resting expenditure are established in other settings, are quantitatively capable of a substantial share, and partially reproduce the gap in simulation41. This is the best-characterised hypothesis, which is not the same as the best-supported: its mediation has never been measured, it cannot explain the prediabetes step, and the model that supports it excluded the main competing hypothesis by construction.
  • Equally compatible with the evidence, far less investigated: blunted anorectic responsiveness (H2) and degraded shared afferent machinery (H3). Both predict everything H1 predicts, plus the prediabetes step that H1 cannot. Both have direct human mechanistic support — failed hypothalamic inhibition after glucose in BMI-matched T2D70, gliosis in IGT and T2D71, loss of GLP-1’s intake effect after human vagotomy94 — and neither has been tested as a mediator of the weight gap. Their lower ranking in earlier drafts reflected the absence of a quantitative model, not the weight of evidence against them.
  • Plausible and quantitatively capable, but unmeasured. Weight-promoting background therapy. Background SGLT2 inhibition. Population composition by sex and age.
  • Weakest. An intrinsic, insulin-resistance-driven pharmacological resistance. Impaired GIP signalling as the primary cause. A reduced endogenous incretin secretory deficit as the primary cause. Note these are weak as primary explanations; the broad, multi-axis version of the afferent hypothesis (H3) is not among them.

12. Unresolved questions and future directions

The central gap is that no study has measured the components of energy balance in people with and without T2D receiving the same incretin agent. A formal modelling analysis has now shown that the energetic offsets of glycaemic correction are capable of partially explaining the gap, and has generated specific, falsifiable predictions41 — but a model that reproduces an observation is not evidence that the modelled mechanism produced it, since competing mechanisms may fit the same trajectories equally well. Direct measurement remains the decisive step, and the author of that analysis says so. Table 4 sets out studies that would resolve the main uncertainties, in order of priority.

Several design principles apply across these studies:

  • Mediation analyses. Studies should prespecify mediation analyses that partition the diabetes difference into intake, expenditure and urinary-loss components, rather than reporting each in isolation.
  • Controlled feeding. Short controlled-feeding studies under matched agonist exposure could separate drug effects on intake from effects on energy disposal.
  • Measurement in T2D trials. Phase 3 programmes in T2D could add urinary glucose collection and expenditure measurement at low cost, which would make these data routine. Baseline autonomic reflex testing and a stored meal-stimulated incretin sample would add similarly little burden and would make studies 7 and 8 answerable retrospectively.
  • Separating pathway from host. Studies 7 and 9 test the peripheral hypothesis of Section 5 in the only way available in humans: by varying vagal integrity rather than diabetes status. Because vagal dysfunction and hyperglycaemia co-vary, either design needs adjustment for HbA1c, and a null result in study 9 would locate the agonist’s anorectic effect centrally and largely close the question.

13. Conclusions

Clinical question. The evidence firmly supports an association between T2D and less weight loss on comparable incretin-based therapy. Across nine matched comparisons spanning six molecules, the placebo-adjusted reduction in T2D was about 50–80% (median ~63%) of that without diabetes, an absolute shortfall of about 2–7 percentage points at 52–72 weeks. The phenotype is not confined to incretin pharmacology: it appears with drugs acting on unrelated targets and after bariatric surgery, which argues against any receptor-level account as the primary cause. The finding is consistent across programmes, in harmonised IPD and in observational cohorts, and it is graded with glycaemic status. Certainty that the association exists is high. Certainty that it reflects effect modification by diabetes itself, rather than co-varying population traits, is moderate at best, because no adjusted cross-programme IPD analysis exists.

Mechanistic question. No mechanism has been measured to explain the gap, and the honest position is that three host-level hypotheses remain live and are not mutually exclusive (Section 11.1): the energetics of glycaemic correction (H1); a generalised deficit in anorectic responsiveness in the diabetic brain (H2); and degradation of the shared multi-axis gut–brain afferent pathway through which satiation signals of every kind must pass (H3).

The energetic account is the best characterised, which should not be mistaken for the best supported. It is quantitatively capable, operates only in the arm whose glycaemia improves, scales with the magnitude of glycaemic correction, and a formal model partially reproduces the gap from glycosuric and expenditure terms alone while predicting that stable background SGLT2 inhibition should produce the greatest attenuation of all41. But its mediation has never been measured; it cannot explain the deficit already present in prediabetes, where glycosuria is negligible; and the model supporting it held appetite parameters fixed at their non-diabetic values, so it excluded the main competing hypothesis by construction rather than testing against it.

The two appetite-and-signalling hypotheses predict everything the energetic account predicts, plus the prediabetes step that it cannot. Both have direct human mechanistic support — hypothalamic inhibition after oral glucose fails in BMI-matched people with T2D70, gliosis is present in impaired glucose tolerance as well as diabetes71, and exogenous GLP-1 loses its effect on food intake after human vagotomy94. Neither has been tested as a mediator of the weight gap. They rank below H1 on how well they are characterised, not on how likely they are.

The generality of the phenotype across drug classes and after bariatric surgery establishes that the cause lies in the host rather than in incretin receptor pharmacology. It does not adjudicate among the three, because all three predict it. Within the peripheral account, the narrow incretin-specific version is weak — incretin secretion is on average preserved in T2D, the portal afferent receptor appears not to be engaged by exogenous agonists, and raising native GLP-1 with DPP-4 inhibition produces no weight loss — but the broad multi-axis version is untouched by those objections. Weight-promoting co-therapies and differences in population composition are plausible additional contributors. Impaired GIP signalling, a primary endogenous secretory deficit, and intrinsic insulin-resistance-driven pharmacological resistance remain the least supported explanations.

Two observations currently discriminate, and they point in opposite directions. The deficit visible in prediabetes favours H2 and H3; the persistence of the gap under enforced, fixed energy intake38 favours H1, though that study carries a baseline composition confounder and an opposing meta-analytic null40. That tension, not a settled answer, is the current state of the field.

The single most important missing piece of evidence is a matched energy-balance study that measures energy intake, total energy expenditure and urinary energy losses in people with and without T2D receiving the same incretin agent, and formally tests which components mediate the difference. Of these, energy intake is the discriminating measurement: equal placebo-adjusted intake suppression with a persisting weight gap would establish H1; smaller suppression in T2D would show that energetics alone cannot be the answer. The modelling gives that study sharper hypotheses — quantitative predictions for urinary glucose excretion, and a falsifiable prediction about background SGLT2 inhibition that existing trials could address by stratified reanalysis alone. Refitting the model’s appetite parameters in T2D, rather than holding them fixed, would be an immediate and inexpensive first test.

Inclusion priorities

Matched-programme RCTs (same molecule/dose/duration/estimand) > harmonised/IPD or single-protocol cohorts > human physiology (clamps, tracers, calorimetry, ad libitum intake, fMRI) > observational cohorts > animal/cellular mechanism.

Peripheral-axis lines searched that yielded nothing usable

These were searched and are reported for completeness; none produced a source that met the inclusion priorities for a claim about diabetes status and weight response to an incretin agonist. - Bile acid / TGR5 signalling and microbiome / short-chain fatty acids as determinants of L-cell secretion in T2D: the retrieved literature concerns metformin’s gut pharmacology and mechanism reviews rather than any comparison relevant to agonist response. Only the metformin-background point was retained. - Enteroendocrine / L-cell density in human T2D intestinal biopsies: no human study comparing L-cell density by diabetes status with a weight or agonist-response endpoint was retrieved. The reduced ileal L-cell number used in Section 5.7 is from a rodent diet-induced obesity model. - Bariatric surgery as a parallel: direct searches did not retrieve a usable matched comparison. Prompted by a reader query about a preprint published after the first draft. Retrieval: - Web search identified Why Do Patients with Type 2 Diabetes Lose Less Weight on GLP-1s? Full text retrieved from the posted preprint HTML after repeated HTTP 429 rate limiting. - Its reference list was then mined and each relevant source resolved by citation matching and verified against its PubMed abstract. This supplied: the STEP UP / STEP UP T2D matched pair, the non-incretin generality evidence (naltrexone-bupropion, lorcaserin, phentermine-topiramate, bariatric surgery, formula diets), the within-T2D HbA1c gradient, and the model’s own inputs (ADAG, renal threshold, the energy-expenditure coefficient). - This round materially changed the review’s conclusions and corrected a sign error in proposed study 6 (see quality_control.md).

Note on preprint retrieval: bioRxiv rate-limits automated access aggressively. Metadata is reliably available through its API; full text required repeated retries. Preprints are not indexed in PubMed, so the PubMed-only strategy declared above would not have found this paper. That is a structural gap in the search, now explicit.

Corrections made while integrating the peripheral-signalling source document (Section 5)

The author-supplied document Peripheral Incretin Signalling in T2DM.docx was verified source by source against PubMed. All 21 of its unique references resolved to PubMed records. Eleven of its claims required correction before use, and these corrections are reflected in Section 5 and logged in evidence/claim_evidence_ledger.csv (C37-C58):

  1. Reduced GLP-1 secretion in T2D was presented as established. Two meta-analyses (22 and 23 trials) find incretin secretion preserved on average in T2D, with reduction predicted by HbA1c and fasting glucose rather than by diabetes status [CALANNA_GLP1, CALANNA_GIP]. Section 5.2 now presents the individual studies and the meta-analyses together, and the graded-with-glycaemia reading replaces the uniform-deficit reading.
  2. DPP-4 “hyperactivity” in metabolic syndrome. Neither cited source establishes that DPP-4 activity is higher than in people without diabetes. Zheng 2015 is cross-sectional within newly diagnosed T2D with no non-diabetic comparator; Malin 2013 is a 12-week exercise intervention in 14 people that measured neither GLP-1 nor body weight. The claim was narrowed to a within-T2D association with insulin resistance.
  3. Willard 2017 was described as pharmacological DPP-4 reduction normalising active GLP-1. It is a genetic neprilysin-deficiency study in mice; lower DPP-4 activity was an associated finding, not an intervention. Reframed as evidence for neprilysin as a second degradative route.
  4. Duchen 1980 was described as “individuals with diabetes”. It is five post-mortem cases of early-onset insulin-dependent (type 1) diabetes with established clinical autonomic failure. Now stated explicitly, with its limited transfer to T2D populations.
  5. Lee 2012 was described as mice. The study used UCD-T2DM rats, compared against prediabetic rats, not “prediabetic control mice”.
  6. Malbert 2021 anatomical site. The reduction in GLP-1 receptor binding potential was between the splenic vein and the entrance of the liver, not “between the porta hepatis and left gastric vein”. Species is not stated in the abstract, so the porcine model is not asserted.
  7. Knauf 2008 receptor dependence. The abolition of the response was demonstrated with central exendin 9-39 infusion and in GLP-1 receptor knockout mice; this is central, not portal, receptor dependence. The finding that high-fat feeding prevented the response entirely was not used in the source document and has been added, being more relevant.
  8. Autonomic neuropathy prevalence. The figure of 18.6% at diagnosis is verified [BONORA2020]. The source document’s “20 to 40% of those with known disease” could not be verified from the cited source and is not used; the reported range is instead given as very wide and test-dependent [AGASHE2018].
  9. The Nishizawa/Edgerton findings cut against the document’s own thesis. The source document states them correctly but treats them as compatible with a peripheral explanation of attenuated weight loss. Because the portal afferent receptor is not engaged by exendin-4 or exenatide, loss of endogenous portal signalling in T2D would lower the baseline rather than blunt the drug. Section 5.4 states this as the principal problem for the hypothesis.
  10. Numeric physiology claims drawn from reviews were dropped. The document’s “50-70% of the total postprandial insulin response”, “more than 50% of an intravenous bolus inactivated within two minutes” and “half-life approximately 1-2 minutes” come from review articles whose abstracts do not contain them. Section 5.1 makes these points qualitatively, citing the reviews, and Section 3 retains the verified primary figures (36% vs 73% incretin contribution [NAUCK1986]).
  11. Hepatoportal emphasis. The document frames the vagal route as hepatoportal. Hayes 2011 shows the common hepatic branch is not required, while complete subdiaphragmatic deafferentation is; the route is therefore described as paracrine signalling on gastrointestinal vagal afferents.

Material added beyond the source document, from new searches: human truncal vagotomy evidence [PLAMBOECK2013]; non-vagal counter-evidence [ZHANGRITTER2011]; route refinement [HAYES2011]; the only agonist-response study with measured peripheral changes [DUCA2013]; the two secretion meta-analyses; neprilysin; background metformin as a confounder acting opposite to the hypothesis [MCCREIGHT2016]; heterogeneity and frequent acceleration of gastric emptying in diabetes [CHEDID2019]; oral semaglutide bioavailability in T2D [OVERGAARD2021PK]; NTS preproglucagon circuit characterisation [HOLT2019].

Reasoning error in the previous draft, corrected (16 October 2026)

Section 2.5 argued: the gap appears with non-incretin drugs and after bariatric surgery, therefore it is not receptor-level, therefore the energetics of glycaemic correction are the explanation. The second inference does not follow. Cross-intervention generality rules out mechanisms specific to incretin receptor pharmacology; it does not select among host-level mechanisms, and at least three are compatible with it:

  1. energetics of glycaemic correction;
  2. a generalised deficit in anorectic responsiveness in the diabetic brain;
  3. degradation of the shared multi-axis gut-brain afferent pathway.

All three predict cross-intervention generality, so that observation cannot discriminate. The error mattered in three places, all now corrected:

  • Section 2.5 previously concluded that generality “raises the weight of explanations rooted in the energetics of the diabetic state.” It now states what the inference does and does not license, and names the three competing classes.
  • Section 5.8 previously used generality to bound the peripheral hypothesis, asserting that “a defect in peripheral incretin signalling cannot explain a deficit that persists when no incretin drug is given.” That is true only of the narrow, incretin-specific version. The vagal afferent pathway described in 5.4-5.6 carries CCK, PYY, ghrelin, distension and nutrient signals, so the broad version predicts exactly the observed generality. The bound has been reversed and the mistake stated in the text.
  • Section 11 and the conclusions previously ranked energetics as uniquely accounting for all robust features of the phenotype. They now distinguish best characterised from best supported, and note that the competing hypotheses additionally explain the prediabetes step, which energetics cannot.

Bariatric surgery was cited as evidence against a receptor-level cause. It is in fact neutral between hypotheses 1 and 3, because RYGB works substantially through large postoperative rises in GLP-1 and PYY and therefore depends on an intact receiving apparatus. This is now stated.

New Section 11.1 sets out the discriminating predictions. A further methodological point is recorded there: the Hall model held appetite parameters fixed at their non-diabetic values, so it could not express hypothesis 2 at all; a blunted appetite response could only appear as residual unexplained gap, which is what the analysis reports. The model therefore cannot distinguish “energetics explains part and nothing else is needed” from “energetics explains part and blunted anorectic response explains the rest.” Table 4 study 3b proposes the direct test (refit the appetite parameters in T2D).

Error in the previous draft, corrected

Proposed study 6 in Table 4 previously argued that co-administering an SGLT2 inhibitor would maintain glycosuria and therefore preserve weight loss in T2D, bringing it closer to non-diabetes levels. This reasoning was wrong and has been corrected. An SGLT2 inhibitor lowers the renal threshold but urinary glucose excretion still scales with plasma glucose above that threshold, so glycaemic improvement reduces UGE regardless — and from a higher baseline, by a larger absolute amount. Hall’s model accordingly predicts the opposite sign: stable background SGLT2i should produce the greatest attenuation. The preprint also distinguishes this from co-initiation of an SGLT2 inhibitor with an incretin agent, which it predicts should produce greater weight loss than either alone. Study 6 now tests all three strata.

Derived values and assumptions

  • Matched-pair ratios (analysis/derived.py): these are ratios of published ETDs. Pairs are not independent (two doses come from the same trials), and no meta-analytic pooling or confidence intervals were computed for the ratios.
  • STEP 1 normoglycaemic estimate (~−15.9%): this assumes the overall mean is the subgroup-size-weighted average of the subgroup means, and it ignores differences in imputation.
  • Energy scenarios (Table 2): glycosuria reductions of 10/25/45 g/day and REE reductions of 0/40/90 kcal/day are assumptions anchored to measured studies in other settings. The conversion uses Hall 2011 (≈23.9 kcal/day per kg at steady state; exponential approach with a 1-year half-time). The scenarios are not predictions for any trial.

Analyses not performed

  • Formal meta-analysis or meta-regression of the T2D × treatment interaction.
  • Any quantitative apportionment of the weight-loss gap to peripheral versus central mechanisms. Section 5 deliberately reaches no numeric estimate, because no input to such a calculation has been measured in either population on treatment.
  • Risk-of-bias assessment (RoB 2) and GRADE certainty rating.
  • Adjustment of matched comparisons for age, sex, BMI or background therapy (requires IPD).
  • Searches of Embase, Cochrane and regulatory documents (FDA/EMA reviews may contain subgroup analyses).

Access limitations

  • Publisher sites (NEJM, Lancet, Wiley, figshare) refused automated access (HTTP 403 or bot challenge) even after network allowlisting.
  • Supplementary appendices for most trials were therefore unavailable.
  • Supplying the PDFs of STEP 2, SURMOUNT-1, REDEFINE 1/2, ATTAIN-1/2 and the STEP-HFpEF trials would allow verification of baseline composition and subgroup analyses (for example, weight response by baseline HbA1c or sulfonylurea use).
