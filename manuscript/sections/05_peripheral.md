## 5. Peripheral incretin signalling: the endogenous background, its degradation and afferent transmission

Section 4 considered the brain. This section considers everything upstream of it: the endogenous incretin secreted by the gut, the enzymes that destroy it, the receptors it meets in the gut wall and hepatoportal bed, and the afferent nerves that carry the resulting signal. Each of these is altered in T2D. Whether any of it changes how a pharmacological agonist performs is a separate question, and the answer turns on a single issue of pharmacology that we take first.

### 5.1 Why the endogenous background might matter at all

A long-acting agonist does not need endogenous GLP-1 to activate the GLP-1 receptor. It reaches sustained plasma concentrations far above physiological ones (Section 3.3), and it is engineered to resist the enzymatic degradation that limits the native peptide [@DRUCKER2018]. On the simplest reading, the state of the endogenous system is therefore irrelevant to drug effect, and reduced native secretion in T2D would predict a *larger* relative response to an agonist, not a smaller one.

Two considerations complicate that reading. First, endogenous GLP-1 acts substantially where it is released. Native GLP-1 has a circulating half-life of minutes, and a large fraction of an intravenous bolus is inactivated before it leaves the portal circulation [@HOLST2007; @BAGGIO2007]. Much of its physiological action is therefore paracrine and neural, exerted on receptors in the gut wall and hepatoportal bed before the peptide reaches the systemic circulation at all [@HOLST2007]. A drug delivered subcutaneously arrives at those same receptors from the systemic side, at different concentrations and without the postprandial timing. Second, the pathway that carries the signal onward — vagal afferent fibres and their brainstem targets — is itself a structure that diabetes damages (Section 5.5). If the drug's effect depends partly on that pathway, then degrading the pathway could degrade the drug effect regardless of receptor occupancy.

The peripheral hypothesis, then, is not that T2D leaves too little endogenous GLP-1 for the drug to work with. It is that T2D may erode a paracrine and neural substrate on which part of the agonist's anorectic effect depends, and may remove a physiological signal that normally works alongside it.

### 5.2 Endogenous GLP-1 and GIP secretion in T2D: contested, and graded with glycaemia

Individual studies support reduced GLP-1 secretion in T2D. In 54 patients with T2D compared with 33 controls with normal glucose tolerance, the 4-hour meal GLP-1 response was lower (area under the curve 2482 ± 145 versus 3101 ± 198 pmol/L × 240 min; p = 0.024), and a model including diabetes, BMI, male sex and other terms explained 42% of the variance in that response [@TOFTNIELSEN2001]. In a meal study of eight obese patients with T2D against eight matched obese healthy participants, GLP-1 responses were reduced in T2D while GIP responses were normal [@VILSBOLL2003].

Systematic evidence qualifies this substantially. A meta-analysis of 22 trials and 29 stimulation tests (275 patients with T2D, 279 controls) found *no* significant difference in total GLP-1 responses by any measure — peak concentration, total or incremental area under the curve, or their time-corrected equivalents [@CALANNA_GLP1]. A companion meta-analysis of 23 trials found GIP secretion likewise preserved [@CALANNA_GIP]. The authors concluded that patients with T2D do not in general show reduced incretin secretion.

The qualification that survives is the one that matters most here. In both meta-analyses, meta-regression showed that HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose predicted the incremental GLP-1 response, and HbA1c predicted GIP responses negatively [@CALANNA_GLP1; @CALANNA_GIP]. Reduced secretion is thus not a property of having diabetes but a function of how poorly controlled it is. This matters for two reasons. It means a uniform secretory deficit cannot be assumed in trial populations, whose mean HbA1c was around 8% (Table 1). And it predicts a graded rather than threshold relationship, which is the shape the clinical phenotype actually has (Section 2.4). The same logic applies at the islet: near-normalisation of glycaemia for four weeks improved the β-cell response to both GLP-1 and GIP three- to fourfold [@HOJBERG2009], and in rat islets chronic hyperglycaemia reduced GLP-1 and GIP receptor expression reversibly [@XU2007].

### 5.3 Degradation: DPP-4 and neprilysin

Dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) cleaves GLP-1(7–36) amide to an inactive form and is expressed on endothelium throughout the portal and systemic circulation [@HOLST2007]. Higher DPP-4 activity would lower the active fraction of endogenous GLP-1 reaching paracrine and portal receptors — a second reduction compounding any secretory deficit.

The human evidence is weaker than it is often presented. In 985 people with newly diagnosed T2D, plasma DPP-4 activity correlated positively with HbA1c and HOMA-IR, and those in the highest activity quartile had worse metabolic profiles [@ZHENG2015]. This is a cross-sectional association within T2D; it does not establish that activity is higher than in people without diabetes. A 12-week exercise and weight-loss intervention in 14 older adults with metabolic syndrome lowered plasma DPP-4 by 10%, and the fall correlated with improved clamp-derived insulin sensitivity (r = −0.59) [@MALIN2013] — an intervention study with no non-diabetic comparator, n = 14, and no measurement of GLP-1 or body weight. Neither study supports a quantitative claim about how much active GLP-1 is lost.

Two mechanistic observations are nonetheless informative about *where* degradation matters. In mice, blood-glucose lowering after the DPP-4 inhibitor anagliptin correlated with small-intestinal DPP-4 inhibition (r = 0.949) but not with plasma DPP-4 inhibition, in both normal and diabetic models, with intact GLP-1 rising in the portal circulation [@YAMASHITA2019]. And neprilysin, upregulated in obesity and T2D, also degrades GLP-1: high-fat-fed neprilysin-deficient mice had higher active GLP-1 (5.8 ± 1.1 versus 3.5 ± 0.8 pmol/L), lower plasma DPP-4 activity and better glucose tolerance [@WILLARD2017]. Both findings locate the degradative bottleneck in the gut and portal bed rather than the systemic circulation. Both are rodent studies, and the neprilysin result is a genetic deletion, not pharmacological inhibition.

The clinical counterpoint is decisive for the drug question. DPP-4 inhibitors raise endogenous active GLP-1 and improve glycaemia, yet produce negligible weight loss. Prolonging the residence of native GLP-1 is therefore not sufficient to reduce body weight, which bounds how much any degradation-based mechanism can contribute to a weight-loss deficit.

### 5.4 Where peripheral GLP-1 acts: paracrine vagal afferents, not the hepatoportal route

GLP-1 receptors are expressed on vagal afferent neurons of the nodose ganglion, which project to the nucleus tractus solitarii and onward to hypothalamic feeding centres [@BAGGIO2007; @KANOSKI2011]. Three lines of animal work define the anatomy more precisely, and they do not all point the same way.

**A portal glucose sensor exists and is GLP-1-receptor-dependent.** Low-rate intragastric glucose in mice increased muscle glycogen synthesis by 30% and induced c-Fos in brainstem and hypothalamus, colocalising with arcuate NPY neurons. The response was diminished by central exendin 9-39 and abolished in GLP-1 receptor knockout mice — and, importantly, high-fat feeding prevented it entirely [@KNAUF2008]. Related work implicates intestinal gluconeogenesis in generating the portal glucose signal detected by periportal afferents [@SOTY2017]. Note that the receptor dependence demonstrated in this model was *central*, not portal.

**But the hepatoportal branch is not the required route.** Selective ablation of the common hepatic branch of the vagus did not abolish the GLP-1-receptor-mediated incretin response or GLP-1's suppression of intake in rats, whereas complete subdiaphragmatic vagal deafferentation did [@HAYES2011]. The authors concluded that what matters is paracrine signalling on GLP-1 receptors borne by vagal afferents of general gastrointestinal origin, not the hepatoportal bed specifically.

**And circulating GLP-1 may not need vagal afferents at all.** Intravenous GLP-1 reduced sucrose intake in rats undiminished by vagotomy or capsaicin treatment — in fact enhanced at the highest dose — with comparable hindbrain c-Fos induction in all groups [@ZHANGRITTER2011]. The authors inferred that vagal participation is limited to paracrine effects near the sites of secretion, and that endocrine GLP-1 acts elsewhere. Conversely, the anorectic and glucose-tolerance effects of the GLP-1-releasing sweetener D-allulose were blunted by vagotomy, GLP-1 receptor blockade and vagal-afferent-selective GLP-1 receptor inactivation [@IWASAKI2018], consistent with a vagal route for *locally released* GLP-1.

The most important finding for the present argument concerns receptor identity. In anaesthetised rats, intraportal GLP-1(7–36) amide at both periphysiological (0.2 pmol) and pharmacological (4.0 pmol) doses increased hepatic vagal afferent discharge. Exendin-4 did not, at any dose up to 40 pmol, and exendin 9-39 at more than 100-fold molar excess failed to block the response to GLP-1(7–36) amide [@NISHIZAWA2000]. The hepatic vagal reception of portal GLP-1 therefore appears to operate through a receptor pharmacologically distinct from the classical GLP-1 receptor — one that a GLP-1 receptor agonist does not engage. Consistent with this, intraportal exenatide in conscious dogs produced no insulin-independent effect on hepatic glucose metabolism [@EDGERTON2013].

This is a problem for the peripheral hypothesis, not a support for it, and it should be stated plainly. If the portal afferent pathway is not activated by exogenous agonists, then loss of endogenous portal signalling in T2D removes a physiological contribution that the drug was never supplying, in both populations. It would not blunt the drug's effect; it would lower the baseline on which the drug acts. The receptor in question has also never been molecularly identified, the experiments are from 2000 in small numbers of anaesthetised rats, and no one has repeated them with a modern long-acting agonist. The claim should be treated as unresolved rather than established.

### 5.5 Human evidence that intact vagal innervation matters

One human study bears directly on whether peripheral neural transmission is necessary for GLP-1's effect on eating. Twenty men with truncal vagotomy and pyloroplasty and ten matched healthy controls received GLP-1(7–36) amide or saline during a standardised meal followed by an ad libitum meal. GLP-1 significantly reduced ad libitum food intake in controls but had no effect in the vagotomised group. It delayed gastric emptying in controls but not after vagotomy, and left insulin secretion unaffected in the vagotomised group while suppressing it in controls. Postprandial glucose fell by the same percentage in both groups, and peak postprandial GLP-1 concentrations were roughly fivefold higher after vagotomy [@PLAMBOECK2013].

This is the strongest available human evidence that intact vagal innervation is required for exogenous GLP-1 to reduce food intake. Its limitations are material. The participants were men with surgical vagotomy and pyloroplasty for ulcer disease, a far more complete and anatomically different lesion than diabetic autonomic neuropathy; pyloroplasty itself accelerates gastric emptying and confounds the intake measurement; the infusion delivered native GLP-1, not a long-acting agonist engineered to act systemically; and the study was not designed to measure body weight over time. It establishes that the vagal route can be rate-limiting for native GLP-1 in humans. It does not establish that partial vagal damage limits the effect of semaglutide or tirzepatide.

### 5.6 Vagal and enteric neuropathy in diabetes

Autonomic neuropathy is common in T2D and begins early. In 806 patients with newly diagnosed T2D undergoing systematic assessment, cardiovascular autonomic neuropathy was present in 18.6% and somatic neuropathy in 21.2% [@BONORA2020]. Reported prevalence across populations and diagnostic methods ranges very widely, from a few per cent to the large majority, depending on the tests applied and the cohort studied [@AGASHE2018]. Cardiovascular autonomic function is a proxy for vagal integrity, not a measure of the gastrointestinal afferent limb.

Direct evidence that the afferent limb is affected is largely preclinical or from type 1 diabetes. A post-mortem study found severe loss of myelinated fibres in the vagus nerve and sympathetic trunks, with ganglion cell changes and inflammatory infiltrates — but in five cases of early-onset *insulin-dependent* diabetes with established clinical autonomic failure [@DUCHEN1980], which limits its transfer to typical T2D trial populations. In db/db mice, morphometric fibre loss and shrinkage appeared across peripheral nerves, though changes in the vagus appeared late relative to other nerves [@ROBERTSON1980].

Two animal studies address function in type 2 models specifically, and they are the most relevant of this group. In UCD-T2DM rats, orogastric glucose activated duodenal enterochromaffin, K and L cells, enteric neurons and vagal afferent neurons in prediabetic animals, but this activation was markedly reduced in recent-onset and 3-month diabetic animals, with reduced GLP-1 immunoreactivity (and preserved GIP) [@LEE2012]. In insulin-resistant obese animals studied with a gallium-labelled GLP-1 receptor PET probe and single-neuron electrophysiology, portal vagal afferents failed to modulate firing during glucose infusion, alongside reduced GLP-1 receptor binding potential, most marked between the splenic vein and the hepatic inlet [@MALBERT2021]. Both show that the gut–vagal glucose-sensing pathway degrades with the diabetic and insulin-resistant state. Neither administered a GLP-1 receptor agonist or measured body weight.

### 5.7 The one study that tests the drug question — and what it does and does not show

Only one study we identified blunted an *agonist's* effect alongside peripheral pathway changes. In obese-prone and obese-resistant rats, intraperitoneal exendin-4 suppressed intake effectively in both strains on chow. On a high-energy, high-fat diet, obese-prone rats suppressed intake significantly less than obese-resistant rats at every dose tested. This was accompanied by downregulated GLP-1 receptor mRNA in the vagal nodose ganglia, lower plasma GLP-1, reduced intestinal GLP-1 protein and a reduced number of L cells in the distal ileum [@DUCA2013].

This is the closest existing analogue of the clinical observation: a dysmetabolic host showing reduced anorectic response to a GLP-1 receptor agonist, with a peripheral mechanism measured in the same animals. It is also a diet-induced obesity model rather than a diabetes model, the agonist is short-acting and given intraperitoneally, the endpoint is acute intake rather than sustained weight change, and the design cannot separate nodose receptor downregulation from the several other changes present.

### 5.8 What the peripheral axis can and cannot carry

Taken together, the peripheral evidence supports a coherent hypothesis and falls well short of establishing it.

In favour: the gut–vagal afferent pathway is measurably degraded in type 2 diabetic and insulin-resistant animals [@LEE2012; @MALBERT2021]; intact vagal innervation is required for native GLP-1 to reduce intake in humans [@PLAMBOECK2013]; agonist-induced anorexia is blunted in a dysmetabolic rodent host with nodose GLP-1 receptor downregulation [@DUCA2013]; and secretory and islet responses recover when glycaemia is corrected [@HOJBERG2009; @CALANNA_GLP1], which fits the graded clinical phenotype.

Against, or limiting: incretin secretion is on average preserved in T2D, with reduction confined to poorer glycaemic control [@CALANNA_GLP1; @CALANNA_GIP]; the portal afferent receptor appears not to be engaged by agonists at all [@NISHIZAWA2000; @EDGERTON2013]; the hepatoportal branch is not the required route even for native GLP-1 [@HAYES2011]; circulating GLP-1 can suppress intake without vagal afferents [@ZHANGRITTER2011]; central GLP-1 receptor populations are sufficient for agonist-induced anorexia in rodents [@SECHER2014; @GABERY2020]; and prolonging native GLP-1 with DPP-4 inhibition does not produce weight loss.

Three further arguments bound the contribution. First, the cross-agent test used in Section 7.3 applies here too. If the deficit depended on the endogenous GLP-1 background or its degradation, it should be smaller for agents that bypass both. Orforglipron is a non-peptide small molecule that is not a DPP-4 substrate, and cagrilintide–semaglutide adds amylin receptor agonism; both show the deficit (ratios 0.71–0.78 and 0.60; Table 1). The attenuation is not confined to agents whose action depends on the native incretin axis. Second, almost all participants in the T2D trials were taking metformin — 89% in SURMOUNT-2 [@SURMOUNT2] — and metformin itself raises GLP-1 concentrations and alters the intestinal bile acid pool and microbiome [@MCCREIGHT2016]. The endogenous background in these populations is pharmacologically modified in the direction opposite to the hypothesised deficit. Third, drug exposure is not reduced in T2D: oral semaglutide bioavailability did not differ significantly between healthy participants and people with T2D, and subcutaneous exposure varies mainly with body weight [@OVERGAARD2021PK; @CARLSSON2018].

The honest position is that the peripheral axis is a well-motivated, mechanistically detailed and almost entirely untested contributor. Its strongest form is not that agonists work less well on diabetic receptors, but that T2D removes a physiological, paracrine and neurally mediated component of satiation that normally operates alongside the drug, and that this loss is graded with glycaemic control. That proposition has never been measured in either population receiving an incretin agent. Section 12 sets out the studies that would test it, including the two most direct: whether baseline autonomic function or measured endogenous incretin secretion predicts weight response to an agonist, and whether the response differs in people with established vagal dysfunction.
