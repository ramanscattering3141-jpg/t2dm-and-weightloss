## 5. Gastrointestinal physiology and gastric emptying

GLP-1 slows gastric emptying acutely. With sustained receptor exposure this effect diminishes. Tachyphylaxis of the delay was evident by a second meal during an 8.5-hour GLP-1 infusion, together with loss of the associated vagal marker pancreatic polypeptide [@NAUCK2011]. A continuous 24-hour infusion attenuated the delay, whereas intermittent exposure preserved it [@UMAPATHYSIVAM2014]. With long-acting agents the picture is mixed. After 20 weeks of semaglutide 2.4 mg, paracetamol absorption showed no delay [@FRIEDRICHSEN2021]. Semaglutide 1.0 mg delayed only first-hour emptying [@HJERPSTED2018]. Exenatide once weekly still slowed scintigraphically measured emptying of solids and liquids at 8 weeks [@JONES2020]. Liraglutide 3.0 mg delayed solid emptying at 5 and 16 weeks in obesity, and the delay at 5 weeks correlated with weight loss at 16 weeks [@HALAWI2017]. Tirzepatide's delay of gastric emptying likewise waned with repeated dosing in healthy participants. In T2D, however, a residual delay persisted during dose escalation, and a long-acting GIP analogue alone did not affect emptying [@URVA2020].

Diabetes affects this axis in ways that could push in either direction. Acute hyperglycaemia slows gastric emptying, as shown in type 1 diabetes under glucose clamp [@FRASER1990]. Diabetic autonomic neuropathy can cause gastroparesis [@CAMILLERI2018]. The glucose-lowering effect of GLP-1 receptor agonism through slower gastric emptying depends on the baseline emptying rate [@PHILLIPS2014; @RAYNER2020]. If emptying is already slow in some people with T2D, the incremental slowing a drug can produce, and any satiation it adds, may be smaller. Conversely, correcting hyperglycaemia could speed emptying, partly offsetting the drug effect. Neither possibility has been tested against weight outcomes.

The weight of evidence suggests that gastric emptying contributes to early satiation and perhaps to variation between individuals in response [@HALAWI2017]. It is not the principal mechanism of long-term weight loss, because weight loss continues after the delay has largely waned, and central receptor populations are sufficient for anorexia in animal models [@SECHER2014; @GABERY2020]. Gastrointestinal changes in T2D are therefore best regarded as a variable and probably minor modifier. They would need to be measured, together with weight, in both populations before they could be assigned any share of the gap. Tolerability, the clinically visible face of gut effects, does not appear worse in T2D trials (Section 2.5).
