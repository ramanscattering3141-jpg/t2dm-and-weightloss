## 13. Conclusions

**Clinical question.** The evidence firmly supports an association between T2D and less weight loss on comparable incretin-based therapy. Across nine matched comparisons spanning six molecules, the placebo-adjusted reduction in T2D was about 50–80% (median ~63%) of that without diabetes, an absolute shortfall of about 2–7 percentage points at 52–72 weeks. The phenotype is not confined to incretin pharmacology: it appears with drugs acting on unrelated targets and after bariatric surgery, which argues against any receptor-level account as the primary cause. The finding is consistent across programmes, in harmonised IPD and in observational cohorts, and it is graded with glycaemic status. Certainty that the *association* exists is high. Certainty that it reflects *effect modification by diabetes itself*, rather than co-varying population traits, is moderate at best, because no adjusted cross-programme IPD analysis exists.

**Mechanistic question.** No mechanism has been measured to explain the gap, and the honest position is that three host-level hypotheses remain live and are not mutually exclusive (Section 11.1): the energetics of glycaemic correction (H1); a generalised deficit in anorectic responsiveness in the diabetic brain (H2); and degradation of the shared multi-axis gut–brain afferent pathway through which satiation signals of every kind must pass (H3).

The energetic account is the **best characterised**, which should not be mistaken for the best supported. It is quantitatively capable in gross terms, operates only in the arm whose glycaemia improves, scales with the magnitude of glycaemic correction, and a formal model partially reproduces the gap from glycosuric and expenditure terms alone while predicting that stable background SGLT2 inhibition should produce the greatest attenuation of all [@HALL2026]. Three things weigh against it. Its mediation has never been measured. It cannot explain the deficit already present in prediabetes, where glycosuria is negligible. And the model supporting it held appetite parameters fixed at their non-diabetic values, so it excluded the main competing hypothesis by construction rather than testing against it.

A fourth consideration may be the most consequential. Energy intake is coupled to energy expenditure — resting metabolic rate is a determinant of the tonic drive to eat, and compensation for an induced energy perturbation grows with intervention duration, approaching high fractions by about 80 weeks [@BLUNDELL2020; @HOPKINS2023; @DOUCET2018; @RIOU2015]. Incretin trials run 68–72 weeks. If compensation in this setting resembles that in the exercise literature, the gross energetic offsets shrink to roughly a sixth of their uncompensated value, leaving about 0.6 kg from the central scenario against an observed gap near 6 kg (Section 9.5). Whether it does transfer is genuinely unknown — the drug is an anorectic, and suppressing the compensatory drive is precisely what it does — but on any reading, the energetic terms cannot simply be summed as independent quantities, and our own scenario arithmetic overstates them by treating them that way.

The two appetite-and-signalling hypotheses predict everything the energetic account predicts, plus the prediabetes step that it cannot, and they gain rather than lose from the coupling argument: if compensation erodes most of the energetic offset, the residual must be carried by something else. Both have direct human mechanistic support — hypothalamic inhibition after oral glucose fails in BMI-matched people with T2D [@VIDARSDOTTIR2007], gliosis is present in impaired glucose tolerance as well as diabetes [@ROSENBAUM2022], and exogenous GLP-1 loses its effect on food intake after human vagotomy [@PLAMBOECK2013]. Neither has been tested as a mediator of the weight gap. They rank below H1 on how well they are characterised, not on how likely they are.

The generality of the phenotype across drug classes and after bariatric surgery establishes that the cause lies in the host rather than in incretin receptor pharmacology. It does not adjudicate among the three, because all three predict it. Within the peripheral account, the narrow incretin-specific version is weak — incretin secretion is on average preserved in T2D, the portal afferent receptor appears not to be engaged by exogenous agonists, and raising native GLP-1 with DPP-4 inhibition produces no weight loss — but the broad multi-axis version is untouched by those objections. Weight-promoting co-therapies and differences in population composition are plausible additional contributors. Impaired GIP signalling, a primary endogenous secretory deficit, and intrinsic insulin-resistance-driven pharmacological resistance remain the least supported explanations.

Two observations currently discriminate, and they point in opposite directions. The deficit visible in prediabetes favours H2 and H3; the persistence of the gap under enforced, fixed energy intake [@HENRY1986] favours H1, though that study carries a baseline composition confounder and an opposing meta-analytic null [@LESLIE2017]. That tension, not a settled answer, is the current state of the field.

**The single most important missing piece of evidence** is a matched energy-balance study that measures energy intake, total energy expenditure and urinary energy losses in people with and without T2D receiving the same incretin agent, and formally tests which components mediate the difference. Of these, **energy intake is the most informative measurement**, though not a clean discriminator on its own: equal placebo-adjusted intake suppression with a persisting weight gap would support H1, and smaller suppression in T2D would show that energetics alone cannot be the answer — but because expenditure drives intake, a measured intake difference may itself be a consequence of the energetic perturbation rather than a primary appetite defect. The three components must be measured together and modelled as a coupled system. Compensation should be measured serially rather than assumed, since it is the largest single uncertainty in the energetic account. The modelling gives that study sharper hypotheses — quantitative predictions for urinary glucose excretion, and a falsifiable prediction about background SGLT2 inhibition that existing trials could address by stratified reanalysis alone. Refitting the model's appetite parameters in T2D, rather than holding them fixed, would be an immediate and inexpensive first test.
