## 13. Conclusions

**Clinical question.** The evidence firmly supports an association between T2D and less weight loss on comparable incretin-based therapy. Across nine matched comparisons spanning six molecules, the placebo-adjusted reduction in T2D was about 50–80% (median ~63%) of that without diabetes, an absolute shortfall of about 2–7 percentage points at 52–72 weeks. The phenotype is not confined to incretin pharmacology: it appears with drugs acting on unrelated targets and after bariatric surgery, which argues against any receptor-level account as the primary cause. The finding is consistent across programmes, in harmonised IPD and in observational cohorts, and it is graded with glycaemic status. Certainty that the *association* exists is high. Certainty that it reflects *effect modification by diabetes itself*, rather than co-varying population traits, is moderate at best, because no adjusted cross-programme IPD analysis exists.

**Mechanistic question.** No mechanism has been *measured* to explain the gap, but one now stands clearly ahead of the others. The energetics of glycaemic correction — retained glycosuric energy plus removal of the excess resting metabolic rate of T2D — operate only in the arm whose glycaemia improves, scale with the magnitude of that correction, and apply to any intervention that lowers weight in diabetes rather than to incretin drugs specifically. A formal energy-balance model fitted to non-diabetic semaglutide response and applied to T2D with appetite parameters held fixed partially reproduces the observed gap from these two terms alone, and predicts that stable background SGLT2 inhibition should produce the greatest attenuation of all [@HALL2026]. That mechanism uniquely accounts for all three robust features of the phenotype: a deficit confined to the drug-attributable component, a gradient with baseline HbA1c, and the appearance of the gap outside incretin pharmacology. Weight-promoting co-therapies and differences in population composition are also plausible contributors. Diminished appetite suppression in T2D is plausible but untested. A peripheral account — in which diabetes erodes the paracrine and vagal afferent machinery that normally delivers a satiation signal alongside the drug — is mechanistically detailed, graded with glycaemic control in the way the clinical phenotype is, and supported by human vagotomy data and by one rodent study in which agonist-induced anorexia was blunted alongside nodose receptor downregulation. It is nonetheless bounded on three sides: incretin secretion is on average preserved in T2D, the portal afferent receptor appears not to be engaged by exogenous agonists, and raising native GLP-1 with DPP-4 inhibition produces no weight loss. Impaired GIP signalling, a primary endogenous secretory deficit, and intrinsic insulin-resistance-driven pharmacological resistance are the least supported explanations.

It remains, however, a modelled explanation rather than a measured one, it is partial on its own account, and it does not explain the deficit already visible in prediabetes, where glycosuria is negligible. A model that reproduces an observation does not establish that the modelled mechanism produced it.

**The single most important missing piece of evidence** is therefore unchanged by the modelling: a matched energy-balance study that measures energy intake, total energy expenditure and urinary energy losses in people with and without T2D receiving the same incretin agent, and formally tests which components mediate the difference in weight loss. The model now gives that study sharper hypotheses to test, including quantitative predictions for urinary glucose excretion and a falsifiable prediction about background SGLT2 inhibition that existing trials could address by stratified reanalysis alone.
