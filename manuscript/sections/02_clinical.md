## 2. Clinical evidence: is weight loss attenuated in T2D?

### 2.1 What kind of comparison can answer the question?

Three kinds of evidence bear on the question, and they differ in strength. The strongest would be a randomised trial that stratifies by diabetes status and formally tests whether the treatment effect differs between strata (effect modification). No such trial has been designed for weight as its primary question. The next best are comparisons in which one protocol, or harmonised protocols, enrolled both populations, ideally analysed together from individual participant data (IPD). Weakest, but most plentiful, are comparisons of separate trials of the same molecule, dose, duration and estimand within one development programme. Comparisons of mean weight change between unrelated trials cannot distinguish an effect of diabetes from differences in population, background therapy or trial conduct. They also cannot show that individual patients respond differently, as opposed to populations differing on average.

### 2.2 Matched programmes

Table 1 summarises the matched comparisons, and Figure 1A shows them graphically. All use treatment-policy or treatment-regimen estimands, which count outcomes regardless of discontinuation, so the comparisons are not distorted by differences in persistence.

For semaglutide 2.4 mg weekly over 68 weeks, STEP 1 (no diabetes; baseline weight 105.3 kg, BMI 37.9 kg/m², HbA1c 5.7%) reported −14.9% versus −2.4% with placebo, an estimated treatment difference (ETD) of −12.4 percentage points [@STEP1]. STEP 2 (T2D; HbA1c 7–10%) reported −9.6% versus −3.4%, an ETD of −6.2 points [@STEP2]. The same pattern recurs at a higher dose in a later pair of trials from the same programme. STEP UP (no diabetes, n=1407; baseline weight 113.0 kg, BMI 39.9 kg/m², 73.7% women) reported −18.7% with semaglutide 7.2 mg versus −3.9% with placebo, an ETD of −14.8 points [@STEPUP]. STEP UP T2D (n=512; baseline weight 110.1 kg, BMI 38.6 kg/m², HbA1c 8.1%, 51.8% women) reported −13.2% versus −3.9%, an ETD of −9.3 points [@STEPUP_T2D]. This is the cleanest matched pair available: same sponsor, dose, duration and estimand, and an *identical* placebo-arm weight change of −3.9% in both populations. For tirzepatide over 72 weeks, SURMOUNT-1 (no diabetes) reported −19.5% at 10 mg and −20.9% at 15 mg versus −3.1% with placebo [@SURMOUNT1]. SURMOUNT-2 (T2D; mean HbA1c 8.02%, diabetes duration 8.5 years) reported −12.8% and −14.7% versus −3.2% [@SURMOUNT2]. For orforglipron 36 mg over 72 weeks the results were −11.2% versus −2.1% without diabetes [@ATTAIN1] and −9.6% versus −2.5% with T2D [@ATTAIN2]. For cagrilintide–semaglutide over 68 weeks they were −20.4% versus −3.0% (ETD −17.3) [@REDEFINE1] and −13.7% versus −3.4% (ETD −10.4) [@REDEFINE2]. Liraglutide 3.0 mg showed the same direction: a placebo-adjusted −5.6 kg without diabetes [@SCALE_OB] and −4.2 kg with T2D [@SCALE_DM], from similar baseline weights (about 106 kg).

Several other programmes point the same way but cannot be matched as cleanly. With retatrutide, a GIP/GLP-1/glucagon receptor tri-agonist, phase 2 weight loss reached 24.2% at 48 weeks in obesity [@RETA_OB] and 16.9% at 36 weeks in T2D [@RETA_DM]. The durations differ, so the comparison is only qualitative. Oral semaglutide 50 mg produced a 15.1% reduction (ETD −12.7) in people without diabetes [@OASIS1]. The corresponding T2D trial of 25 and 50 mg had no placebo arm [@PIONEERPLUS]. In the SURPASS and SUSTAIN programmes in T2D, doses were chosen for glycaemic control and comparators were active. Weight reductions were 7.0–9.5 kg with tirzepatide monotherapy over 40 weeks [@SURPASS1], 1.9–5.5 kg greater with tirzepatide than with semaglutide 1 mg [@SURPASS2], and only 0.9 kg greater with semaglutide 2.0 mg than with 1.0 mg [@SUSTAINFORTE]. These T2D trials show that weight response rises with dose and potency in diabetes. They do not provide a matched comparator without diabetes.

Across the nine percentage-based comparisons, the placebo-adjusted reduction in T2D was a median 0.63 times that without diabetes (range 0.50–0.78). The absolute deficit was a median 5.5 percentage points (range 1.8–6.9) (Table 1; analysis/derived.py). Two features stand out. First, the ratio is broadly similar for selective GLP-1 receptor agonists, the GIP/GLP-1 co-agonist tirzepatide and the amylin–GLP-1 combination. Second, placebo-arm weight change was not smaller in T2D (−3.4% vs −2.4% for semaglutide 2.4 mg; −3.9% vs −3.9% for semaglutide 7.2 mg; −3.2% vs −3.1% for tirzepatide; −2.5% vs −2.1% for orforglipron; −3.4% vs −3.0% for cagrilintide–semaglutide). The deficit therefore lies in the drug-attributable component rather than in the response to the lifestyle programme delivered to both arms.

### 2.3 Harmonised and single-protocol evidence

The STEP-HFpEF programme provides the closest available approach to a direct test. Two trials with harmonised eligibility criteria, endpoints and analyses enrolled people with obesity-related heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, one without diabetes and one with T2D. Both gave semaglutide 2.4 mg for 52 weeks. Weight change was −13.3% versus −2.6% without diabetes (ETD −10.7) [@STEPHFpEF] and −9.8% versus −3.4% with T2D (ETD −6.4) [@STEPHFpEF_DM]. The prespecified IPD pooled analysis found substantial heterogeneity between the trials for weight (p<0.0001; I² 95.8%), whereas improvements in heart-failure symptoms were consistent [@HFpEF_POOL]. The authors note roughly 40% less weight loss with diabetes. Even here, diabetes status was not randomised, and the trials differed in recruitment and background therapy.

The phase 2 trial of maridebart cafraglutide, which combines GLP-1 receptor agonism with GIP receptor antagonism, enrolled an obesity cohort and an obesity-with-T2D cohort under one protocol [@MARITIDE2025]. At 52 weeks, weight change ranged from −12.3% to −16.2% (placebo −2.5%) in the obesity cohort and from −8.4% to −12.3% (placebo −1.7%) in the T2D cohort. The dosing arms were not identical between cohorts, and the T2D cohort was older (55.1 vs 47.9 years) and included fewer women (42% vs 63%).

Observational data point the same way. In a referral-centre cohort treated with semaglutide 1.7–2.4 mg, people with T2D lost 7.2% versus 11.8% at 6 months [@GHUSN2022]. In a large propensity-matched electronic-record cohort, people without T2D lost more weight than people with T2D on both tirzepatide and semaglutide. The authors explicitly raise differences in motivation and concurrent weight-loss activity as possible explanations [@RODRIGUEZ2024].

### 2.4 A gradient with dysglycaemia

The gradient also appears *within* T2D, indexed by glycaemic control rather than by diabetes status. In SURMOUNT-2, weight reduction with tirzepatide varied meaningfully by baseline HbA1c category [@SATTAR2025], and pooled patient-level analyses of the SUSTAIN programme found baseline characteristics including glycaemia to modify the weight response to semaglutide [@ARODA2020]. The direction is consistent: higher baseline HbA1c, smaller weight reduction. This within-disease gradient is the single most informative feature of the phenotype, because it is the common prediction of every mechanism that scales with glycaemia — the energetic offsets of Section 9 above all — and it is not predicted by an explanation resting on diabetes status as a category.

In a post hoc analysis of STEP 1, participants with prediabetes lost 13.7% with semaglutide (ETD −11.3) [@PERREAULT2022], compared with 14.9% in the whole semaglutide arm [@STEP1]. Assuming the overall mean is the weighted average of the subgroup means, normoglycaemic participants lost about 15.9%. Normoglycaemia (~15.9%), prediabetes (13.7%) and T2D (9.6%) therefore form a graded series on the same dose and duration (Figure 1B). The gradient is unadjusted and comes from different trials, so it does not establish dose–response causation. It does argue against a threshold effect confined to overt diabetes. Its presence in prediabetes, where glycosuria is negligible, also limits how much of the gap any glycosuria-based explanation alone can carry (Section 9). In SURMOUNT-1, participants with prediabetes still lost 18.7–19.7% with tirzepatide 10–15 mg over 176 weeks [@SURMOUNT1_3Y], so large responses remain achievable in dysglycaemia.

### 2.5 The gap is not specific to incretin drugs

A phenotype confined to incretin pharmacology would point to the receptor. A phenotype that appears whenever weight is lost by any means points to the host. The evidence favours the second, and this bears directly on which mechanisms in Sections 5 to 10 can be primary.

Smaller weight reductions in T2D have been reported with three non-incretin pharmacotherapies acting on entirely unrelated targets. Naltrexone–bupropion produced −5.0% versus −1.8% with placebo in T2D (ETD −3.2) [@HOLLANDER2013], against larger reductions in the non-diabetes COR programme [@GREENWAY2010]. Lorcaserin, a 5-HT2C agonist, produced −4.5% to −5.0% versus −1.5% in BLOOM-DM (ETD about −3.0) [@ONEIL2012], again below the non-diabetes BLOOM result [@SMITH2010]. Phentermine–topiramate shows the same direction [@GADDE2011]. Bariatric surgery likewise: preoperative diabetes is a recognised predictor of smaller long-term weight loss [@COURCOULAS2015]. None of these acts on the GLP-1 or GIP receptor, and surgery is not a drug at all.

Dietary restriction is the informative exception, and it cuts both ways. In a 36-day metabolic-ward study at a fixed 300 kcal/day, participants with T2D lost 308 ± 19 g/day versus 450 ± 31 g/day in non-diabetic participants (p<0.01) — but the non-diabetic group began with significantly greater body weight and lean mass, which the authors note as a confounder [@HENRY1986]. Behavioural programmes showed the same direction [@WING1987]. Against this, a systematic review and meta-analysis of five low-energy formula-diet studies (n=569) found *no* significant difference in final weight loss between people with and without T2D: −1.2 kg (95% CI −4.1 to 1.6), with near-identical rates of loss (0.6 vs 0.5 kg/week) [@LESLIE2017].

Two readings of that null are possible. It may mean the gap is specific to pharmacotherapy and surgery and absent under enforced energy restriction. Or the meta-analysis may be underpowered and dominated by individual studies, as has been argued [@HALL2026]. We cannot settle this from the published data. What matters for the present argument is the asymmetry: the gap appears across drug classes with no shared receptor and after surgery, which makes a receptor-level or incretin-signalling defect an unlikely *primary* cause, and correspondingly raises the weight of explanations rooted in the energetics of the diabetic state itself [@BAYS2023].

Note that the Henry study is also a direct, if small, demonstration of the energetic mechanism at work: fasting glucose fell from 291 to 95 mg/dL in the diabetic participants over the 36 days, closing a large glycosuric energy leak, at fixed intake (Section 9).

### 2.6 Could the difference be an artefact?

Several features of the trials argue against a purely methodological explanation.

* **Dose, duration and estimand** were matched within each programme.
* **Discontinuation and tolerability** do not favour the trials without diabetes. Gastrointestinal adverse events were reported by 72.5% in REDEFINE 2 versus 79.6% in REDEFINE 1 [@REDEFINE1; @REDEFINE2]. Discontinuations for adverse events were similar in ATTAIN-1 and ATTAIN-2 (5.3–10.3% vs 6.1–9.9%) [@ATTAIN1; @ATTAIN2].
* **Analyses that remove discontinuation** leave the difference in place. Under the efficacy estimand, which estimates the effect had treatment continued as intended, SURMOUNT-2 reported ETDs of −10.1 and −12.4 points [@SURMOUNT2]. These remain well below the treatment-regimen ETDs of −16.4 and −17.8 points in SURMOUNT-1 [@SURMOUNT1], although the treatment-regimen estimand includes the effect of discontinuation and so understates the drug's full on-treatment effect.
* **Drug exposure** is unlikely to explain the difference. Within T2D programmes, population pharmacokinetic analyses identify body weight as the only clinically relevant covariate of semaglutide exposure [@CARLSSON2018], and weight and HbA1c responses track plasma exposure [@OVERGAARD2021]. Baseline body weights in the matched programmes were similar. An exposure–response model of semaglutide spanning populations with and without T2D used baseline HbA1c as a covariate [@STRATHE2023], but we could not verify from the accessible text how much it changed the predicted response.

Other differences do favour the trials without diabetes. The T2D trials enrol older participants with a higher proportion of men. Women lost more weight in the STEP-HFpEF pool [@HFpEF_POOL]; STEP 1 enrolled 74.1% women and SURMOUNT-2 50.7% [@STEP1; @SURMOUNT2]. T2D participants also continued glucose-lowering drugs, including sulfonylureas in 26.7% of SURMOUNT-2 participants [@SURMOUNT2]. No analysis has pooled IPD across programmes with adjustment for age, sex, baseline BMI, HbA1c, diabetes duration and background therapy. Without one, the share of the gap attributable to these population differences cannot be quantified.

### 2.7 What the clinical evidence establishes

The clinical phenotype is robust. It appears in every matched programme, in harmonised IPD, in a single-protocol trial and in observational cohorts, and it is graded with glycaemic status. On average, people with T2D achieve about 50–80% (median ~60%) of the placebo-adjusted weight reduction seen without diabetes. The absolute shortfall is about 2–7 percentage points at 52–72 weeks, larger with the most potent agents. Because the deficit occurs in the drug-attributable component, and across GLP-1 receptor agonists, GIP receptor agonism and antagonism, amylin co-agonism and lifestyle intervention [@WING1987], it is more likely to reflect host physiology and context than a defect specific to one receptor. What the evidence cannot yet establish is how much of the population difference is effect modification by diabetes itself rather than confounding by co-varying traits.
