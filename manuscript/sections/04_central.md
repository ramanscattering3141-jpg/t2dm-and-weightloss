## 4. Central regulation of appetite and energy intake

### 4.1 How incretin agonists reduce intake

Reduced energy intake is the dominant driver of incretin-induced weight loss. In adults with obesity, semaglutide 1.0 mg reduced ad libitum energy intake across a test day by 24%, with less hunger and food craving, better control of eating and a lower preference for high-fat foods. Resting metabolic rate adjusted for lean mass did not change [@BLUNDELL2017]. Semaglutide 2.4 mg reduced ad libitum lunch intake by 35% after 20 weeks [@FRIEDRICHSEN2021]. Tirzepatide reduced intake at an ad libitum lunch by about 525 kcal at week 3 in adults without diabetes. It also reduced food cravings, the tendency to overeat and responsiveness to food cues, and reduced activation to high-fat, high-sugar food images in orbitofrontal, cingulate and hippocampal regions [@MARTIN2025]. In humans, GLP-1 receptors have been identified on neurons of the hypothalamus, medulla and parietal cortex [@FARR2016].

Animal studies define the circuitry. Peripherally administered semaglutide does not cross the blood–brain barrier. It reaches circumventricular organs and periventricular sites, activates hindbrain and hypothalamic neurons, and engages secondary nodes such as the lateral parabrachial nucleus. In rodents it reduces intake without lowering energy expenditure [@GABERY2020]. Liraglutide directly activates arcuate POMC/CART neurons and indirectly inhibits NPY/AgRP neurons through GABAergic transmission. Its weight-lowering effect persisted after loss of GLP-1 receptors in vagal afferents, area postrema or paraventricular nucleus [@SECHER2014]. Other work shows that vagal afferent and central receptor populations both contribute, because vagal deafferentation shifts the dose–response curve [@KANOSKI2011]. The pharmacological effect is also separable from the endogenous brainstem preproglucagon system; those neurons are not required for the anorexia induced by GLP-1 receptor agonists [@BRIERLEY2021]. These rodent data describe pathways. They do not show that any pathway behaves differently in human T2D.

### 4.2 Appetite suppression in T2D: present, magnitude untested

Three types of evidence should be kept apart.

**Incretin agonism suppresses appetite in T2D.** This is well supported. Intravenous GLP-1 reduced energy intake by 27% in T2D [@GUTZWILLER1999]. Liraglutide 1.8 mg reduced ad libitum intake by 18% after 3 weeks [@FLINT2013]. Tirzepatide and semaglutide both reduced appetite in T2D over 28 weeks [@HEISE2023]. In a crossover fMRI study, exenatide reduced food intake and food-cue responses in insula, amygdala, putamen and orbitofrontal cortex in obese people with T2D as well as in normoglycaemic obese people, and receptor blockade with exendin 9-39 largely prevented these effects [@VANBLOEMENDAAL2014]. In T2D, liraglutide reduced insula and putamen responses to food pictures after 10 days but not after 12 weeks, relative to insulin glargine [@TENKULVE2015]. It also reduced parietal activation to highly desirable foods [@FARR2016].

**Whether appetite suppression is smaller in T2D** is unknown. No study has compared ad libitum intake, appetite ratings or food-cue responses between people with and without diabetes receiving the same agonist under identical conditions. Apparent differences between studies, such as 27% with GLP-1 infusion in T2D versus 12% in healthy men [@FLINT1998], reflect different infusion rates, meals and populations and cannot be interpreted as effect modification.

**Whether a difference in appetite suppression explains the weight-loss gap** has therefore never been tested. One observation is suggestive. In T2D, tirzepatide produced more weight loss than semaglutide 1 mg, but intake at an ad libitum lunch did not differ between the drugs. The authors concluded that measured intake did not account for the difference in weight outcome [@HEISE2023]. A single test meal is an insensitive measure of 24-hour intake, so this finding should not be overinterpreted.

### 4.3 Could the diabetic state blunt central responses?

Several plausible mechanisms exist, but none has been linked to incretin response. In adults with obesity, MRI markers of mediobasal hypothalamic gliosis were greater in impaired glucose tolerance and T2D than in normal glucose tolerance, independent of adiposity. They correlated with glycaemia and insulin resistance [@ROSENBAUM2022]. Brain insulin resistance in obesity and T2D alters eating behaviour and reward processing [@KULLMANN2016]. In obese T2D and normoglycaemic obese participants, food-cue responses in appetite and reward regions were greater than in lean participants [@VANBLOEMENDAAL2014]. A larger baseline drive to eat would oppose a given pharmacological suppression. Whether gliosis, central insulin resistance or heightened food-cue reactivity reduces the *magnitude* of GLP-1-induced anorexia in T2D has not been studied. These remain hypotheses.

### 4.4 Compensatory regulation

Weight loss provokes compensatory increases in appetite. In patients treated with canagliflozin, whose energy deficit comes from glycosuria without appetite suppression, calculated energy intake rose by about 100 kcal/day for each kilogram lost. That is more than three times the accompanying adaptation in energy expenditure [@POLIDORI2016]. A common interpretation, not directly tested in humans, is that incretin agonists shift the level at which this feedback operates. Under that model, any additional, drug-independent energy inflow in T2D, such as glucose energy previously lost in urine (Section 8), would act on this feedback system and lead to a higher weight plateau.
