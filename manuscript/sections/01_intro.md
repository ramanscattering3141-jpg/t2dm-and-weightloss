## 1. Introduction

Medicines that act on the glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor were first developed to lower glucose. They amplify glucose-dependent insulin secretion and suppress glucagon. They later proved to be the most effective drug treatments for obesity, and newer molecules add activity at the glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP), glucagon or amylin receptors [@DRUCKER_HOLST2023; @DRUCKER2025]. The same drugs are therefore used for two related but distinct goals: correcting dysglycaemia and reducing adiposity.

A consistent observation accompanies this dual use. When one molecule is studied at the same dose and for the same duration in separate trials of people with and without T2D, the trials in T2D report smaller weight reductions. For semaglutide 2.4 mg the placebo-adjusted reduction at 68 weeks was 12.4 percentage points without diabetes [@STEP1] and 6.2 points with T2D [@STEP2]. Similar differences appear with tirzepatide [@SURMOUNT1; @SURMOUNT2], with the oral small-molecule agonist orforglipron [@ATTAIN1; @ATTAIN2] and with cagrilintide–semaglutide [@REDEFINE1; @REDEFINE2].

The difference matters clinically, because weight reduction of this size changes glycaemic, cardiovascular and functional outcomes in T2D, and smaller responses shape both expectations and choice of agent. It is also informative mechanistically. Glucose lowering and weight reduction are separate pharmacological outputs. Correcting hyperglycaemia depends mainly on islet effects, whereas weight reduction depends on a sustained negative energy balance. The mechanisms that correct dysglycaemia need not determine weight loss, and correcting dysglycaemia can itself change energy balance.

This review addresses two questions separately. The clinical question is how confidently we can conclude that T2D is associated with less weight loss on comparable incretin-based therapy, and how large the difference is. The mechanistic question is which physiological or pharmacological processes could explain the difference, and which have actually been shown to do so. We consider four classes of explanation: (1) direct modification by T2D of the response to receptor agonism; (2) indirect metabolic effects of glycaemic correction on energy balance; (3) clinical and behavioural factors, including co-prescribed medicines; and (4) methodological artefacts arising from comparisons between trials. Throughout, we distinguish established causal links, supportive human evidence, indirect inference and hypotheses.

The evidence was assembled through structured searches of PubMed/MEDLINE and ClinicalTrials.gov, with backward citation tracking from pivotal trials. Every quantitative claim was checked against the primary abstract or, where accessible, the full text. This is a narrative review; it is not a systematic review and makes no claim of exhaustive screening (see Supplementary Methods and the quality-control report).
